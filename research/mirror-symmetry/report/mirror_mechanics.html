Mirror Mechanics

Why price action so often retraces its own path, why the retrace is usually faster than the move it undoes, and which parts of “levels” survive contact with data. A theory built from the ideal double top, tested on Nasdaq order-level data and 57 assets, then applied to SPY in May and November 2026.

What the evidence says

The observation

In May 2026 SPY climbed from 718.66 to 756.48 (raw closes, Apr 30 to May 29). From June to October it ranged above that band. Between 12 and 19 November it fell back through the whole band, and on 20 November it broke below it to 689.81. Played backwards, November looks like May. It also took a quarter of the time.

Starting from the ideal double top

The ideal double top makes three separate symmetry claims, and each one maps to a different mechanism.

  1. Level symmetry. The second peak T₂ stops at the first peak T₁. Liquidity story: buyers trapped at T₁ sell at breakeven (overhead supply), and resting offers sit there.
  2. Leg symmetry. Each leg retraces the previous one through the same prices. Liquidity story: positions opened on the way up are closed on the way down, roughly last-in, first-out, because the latest buyers have the thinnest cushion.
  3. Measured move. After the neckline N breaks, the fall equals the pattern height. Liquidity story: stops cluster under the neckline, and below it lies whatever the earlier rally left behind, thick or thin.

To turn this into science, each claim has to become a property of a model, and the model has to say when the symmetry should fail.

The theory

Treat log price as a particle moving through a landscape that depends on the price level, plus forces that depend on the path. Price levels enter through the drift b (barriers, overhead supply, dip demand) and through the local speed σ, which is inverse to liquidity: the Kyle relation Δp = λ·(net flow), with λ ∝ 1/depth.

$$dp_t \;=\; \underbrace{b(p_t)\,dt + \sigma(p_t)\,dW_t}_{\text{landscape: level-dependent}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mu_t\,dt + (\sigma_t - 1)\,\sigma(p_t)\,dW_t}_{\text{path-dependent: leverage, stops, news}}$$
Theorem 1 · Reversibility (the mirror is generic)

For any one-dimensional diffusion whose drift and volatility depend only on the price level, the transition density is symmetric with respect to the speed measure m:

$$m(x)\,p_t(x,y) \;=\; m(y)\,p_t(y,x), \qquad m(x)=\frac{2}{\sigma^2(x)}\exp\!\Big(\int^x \frac{2\,b(u)}{\sigma^2(u)}\,du\Big).$$

Consequences: (a) the path from a to b, reversed in time, has the same law as a path from b to a, so the return leg is statistically the mirror of the outgoing leg; (b) up-crossings and down-crossings of a band have the same duration distribution; (c) a constant drift does not break this, because drifts +μ and −μ are Doob h-transforms of each other and give identical bridges. The landscape can be as rich as you like and the mirror still comes out.

Theorem 2 · The speed profile belongs to the level

The expected time a crossing of [a, b] spends near level y is the Green function of the conditioned process times the speed density. Without drift (natural scale):

$$\mathbb{E}\big[\text{time near } y\big] \;\propto\; \frac{(y-a)(b-y)}{b-a}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma^2(y)} \quad\text{for both directions.}$$

So where a static landscape is slow (deep liquidity), both legs linger; where it is thin, both legs rush. This is the precise version of “the space where price action mirrors in reverse.”

Theorem 3 · The variance clock (Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz)

Any continuous martingale run on its own quadratic-variation clock is a Brownian motion: pt − p0 = W⟨p⟩t. Measured in realized variance, crossings are symmetric whatever the volatility does. So:

  • asymmetry that disappears in variance time comes from path-dependent volatility (leverage effect, stop cascades, dealer hedging);
  • asymmetry that survives in variance time comes from path-dependent drift (momentum, regime-dependent mean reversion);
  • asymmetry cannot come from levels alone (Theorem 1).

Barriers, ranges and breakouts

A level that repels price is a hump in the potential U with b = −U′. Escape over a hump of height ΔU follows Kramers’ law: mean time to break ∝ exp(2ΔU/σ²). Thick levels make long ranges and repeated tests; higher volatility breaks them sooner. If each test consumes the breakeven supply, ΔU falls with every touch (depletion); if trading at the level writes more liquidity, ΔU rises (reinforcement). The data have to decide which.

Microstructure foundation

At the order-book level, the landscape is real resting and latent liquidity. Price moves until net aggressive flow is absorbed: Δp ≈ λ·OFI, with λ ∝ 1/depth (Kyle 1985; Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov 2014). No-dynamic-arbitrage forces the uninformed part of impact to be reversible (Huberman & Stanzl 2004; Gatheral 2010), otherwise a round-trip trade would manipulate price for profit. Reversible impact makes price behave like a state function of net inventory: when the flow that built a move unwinds, the move unwinds through the same prices. That is the mirror. The irreversible part is information, and it is what makes some moves permanent.

Non-horizontal levels

The landscape is written by the path and decays: its ridges are the cost bases of cohorts. A cohort that keeps adding as price trends has a cost basis that moves with it, the anchored VWAP or a turnover-weighted reference price (Grinblatt & Han 2005). So the same theory produces horizontal levels (a cohort that traded in a range) and sloping ones (a cohort that accumulated along a trend). Both are ridges of one evolving field.

Predictions

The theory makes the falsifiable predictions below. The last column is the verdict from the experiments that follow.

Experiment 1 · Is the mirror distorted, and by what?

For each asset, I tiled the log-price range with bands 12 typical daily moves wide and timed every crossing, counting only time spent inside the band. Δ is the mean log duration of down-crossings minus up-crossings: −1 means declines through the same prices take e−1 ≈ 37% as long as rallies. The null keeps each asset’s exact volatility path and randomizes the signs of returns (200 surrogates).

Experiment 2 · Do levels exist, and are double tops special?

Barrier test. After every swing high (4 daily σ zigzag), I took the first return to within 0.5σ of it and raced ±2σ from there: reject or break. Placebo levels at random offsets below the pivot went through the same procedure. A real double-top effect would push swing highs above placebo.

Landscape test. A causal regression of each day’s volatility-normalized range on the share of past volume traded at that price, with drawdown and leverage controls. Volume at a price slows later traversal across asset classes. Built only from volume at least 250 days old, SPY gives β = −0.021 (z = −4.7 against random signs). A calibrated model with all the path dependence and no levels fakes about half of that (−0.0095 ± 0.004). The excess, about −0.01, is the part attributable to genuine levels: suggestive, not conclusive, and small (roughly 5% slower through the heaviest nodes).

Experiment 3 · The order book on 15 and 18 May 2026

From Nasdaq’s TotalView-ITCH 5.0 files for 15 and 18 May 2026 (two days inside the May rally), I replayed every add, execute, cancel, replace and hidden execution: six symbols on 15 May and three on 18 May, 12.4 and 16.2 million messages for SPY alone. The rebuilt books match the sessions. 15 May: open 741.85, high 743.46, low 737.965, close 739.09 against the official 741.79 / 743.46 / 737.96 / 739.17. 18 May: 739.72 / 741.405 / 733.445 / 738.555 against 739.83 / 741.42 / 733.39 / 738.65.

Round-number depth: displayed depth at whole-dollar prices vs other prices at the same distance from the mid, as a multiple (exp of the log coefficient). Crossing clocks: Δ for 20¢ intraday bands, down minus up. Hysteresis: mean |Δmid| over 5 minutes when Nasdaq net flow returned to its start (lowest quintile) vs when it did not (highest quintile).

Three results held in all nine symbol-days: price moves toward the thinner queue, whole-dollar prices carry far more resting depth, and price moves less when net flow returns. Business-time symmetry did not hold everywhere. On 15 May the clock asymmetry vanished in volume time; on 18 May, a day with a sharp intraday selloff, SPY and QQQ declines were faster in volume and trade time too, which matches the daily-scale finding that declines need less volume per cent. Nasdaq-only flow explains between 0% and 27% of 5-minute price changes, so the state-function reading of impact is only partly visible from one venue.

Model check · which ingredient makes which fact

The landscape diffusion was simulated with ingredients switched on one at a time (12 paths × 9,000 days each) and measured with exactly the statistics used on real data. Read across a row to see what an ingredient produces. The bottom row is SPY.

The variance clock in this table weights the partial first and last step of a crossing by time. The stricter distance-weighted clock gives SPY +0.40 against +0.14 for the leverage-only model; the conclusion is the same.

A static landscape produces a perfect mirror: symmetric durations and mirrored speed profiles (row A), as Theorem 1 says. Leverage alone produces most of the distortion seen in real equities (row F). Dense barriers would raise rejection rates above the null everywhere (rows B–C), and real data do the opposite. Only path dependence plus a landscape matches SPY on all four measures. One caution came out of this table: path dependence alone can produce a positive speed-profile mirror (row G), so that statistic alone is weak evidence for levels.

SPY, May vs November 2026

The band is [718.66, 756.48]: the raw closes that start and end the May rally. May crossed it upward from 4 May 15:30 to 29 May 09:30. November crossed it downward from 12 Nov 15:30 to 19 Nov 14:30 (hourly bars, raw prices, in-band time only).

Verdict on the example. Three things held: the same prices were retraced; the 3.6× time compression is in line with SPY’s historical average (Δ = −1.39, about 4×); and most of it is the volatility clock (0.28 on hours, 0.68 on variance). One thing did not: the detailed speed profile inside the band was not mirrored, and an individual pair cannot show that anyway (a single pair’s mirror correlation has a null standard deviation of 0.43). The pause at May’s swing lows is suggestive but anecdotal: across 57 assets, swing levels hold no better than random ones.

Pre-registered predictions for SPY

Stated on Saturday 21 Nov 2026 after the 689.81 close, before any of it can be checked. Base rates come from every down-crossing of a 9.3% band (the width of the 757.12 → 689.81 decline) in SPY since 1993 and the S&P 500 since 1928, conditioned on fast declines (8 trading days or fewer through the band; November took 6).

What is tradable

  • The mirror shape is not an edge. Theorem 1 says any level-dependent random process produces mirrors, and two random paths through the same band look alike by construction. Pattern-matching shapes buys nothing on its own.
  • Time is the edge. In equities the distortion is large and stable: rallies are undone 2–4× faster than they were built, and recoveries from fast drops take about 3× the drop. Size time stops and option tenors to that: retracement risk on a rally is front-loaded; a recovery bet after a crash needs time.
  • Use the variance clock to project a mirror. Expected time to retrace ≈ variance the original move used × (0.75 for an up-mirror after a decline, about 1.3–1.5 for a down-mirror of a rally) ÷ forecast daily variance.
  • Levels: prior swing highs and lows are not better barriers than random prices at the daily scale. Old high-volume zones slow price modestly. Round numbers concentrate resting liquidity and new orders, which matters for where to rest limit orders and where stops get run.
  • Book imbalance predicts the next tick, but that edge lives at high-frequency latencies and costs.
  • Where mirrors are symmetric (Treasuries, EUR/USD), time-symmetric mean-reversion rules are internally consistent. In equities they are not.

Research, not investment advice. Base rates are historical frequencies, not guarantees.

Data, methods, limits

  • Data. Yahoo Finance daily OHLCV for 57 assets (indices from 1928, ETFs, 20 stocks, bonds, commodities, FX, crypto, VIX), dividend-unadjusted for level work. SPY hourly bars (Dec 2023–Nov 2026) and 1-minute bars (Oct 23–Nov 20 2026); Yahoo’s 1-minute volume does not reconcile with daily volume, so only shares within a day were used. Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH 5.0 for 15 May 2026 (SPY, QQQ, IWM, AAPL, NVDA, TSLA) and 18 May 2026 (SPY, QQQ, NVDA), Nasdaq venue only.
  • Nulls. Sign-randomized returns keep the exact volatility path, so volatility clustering cannot fake a result; they also remove leverage, which is why the calibrated no-landscape model was added as a second, stronger null.
  • Limits. Two ITCH days; Nasdaq is a minority of SPY volume, so flow-to-price R² is a lower bound. No option open-interest data, so dealer-gamma levels are untested. Cross-asset tests overlap (SPY, DIA, S&P 500), so pooled significance is overstated. The no-landscape null was calibrated by hand to SPY’s calendar asymmetry.
