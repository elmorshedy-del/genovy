# Cognitive outcomes of ECT for major depression: lasting (>6 months) versus transient impairment

Scope note for the report writer: these notes cover magnitude, domains, time course and persistence of ECT cognitive effects. Each finding is tagged **[GROUP]** (group means / pooled effect sizes) or **[INDIV]** (individual-level: reliable change, % of patients, subgroups), plus the design. "SMD/g/d" = standardized mean differences; negative = worse than baseline unless stated otherwise. Tools: Tavily and Exa were used for search and extraction. The Scite MCP needed a paid plan and returned an error, so retraction and correction checks were not run systematically (see Gaps). One corrigendum was found incidentally (Kolshus 2017, below). Two corrections to the brief: the Semkovska & McLoughlin 2010 DOI is 10.1016/j.biopsych.2010.06.009, and Landry et al. 2021 appeared in *J ECT*, not *Neuropsychology Review*.

## 1. What do the major meta-analyses show by time point (0–3 days, ~2 weeks, 1–6 months, >6 months)?

### Takeaway
For anterograde memory, processing speed, attention, working memory and executive function, pooled data show impairment mainly in the first 0–3 days and occasionally up to 2–4 weeks for verbal recall and fluency. By about 2 weeks to 1 month, scores return to baseline, and by 1–6 months they are often above baseline as depression lifts. The exception is retrograde autobiographical memory. The newest meta-analyses (2025–2026) find that autobiographical memory loss does **not** recover between end of treatment and 6–12 months. Its size depends strongly on technique: small with right unilateral (RUL) ECT, large with bilateral ECT. Pooled data beyond 6 months are thin for every domain.

### Cited Findings

**Time-point summary (group-level, pooled)**

**Individual meta-analyses**
- **Semkovska & McLoughlin 2010, *Biol Psychiatry* 68:568–577.** 84 within-subject studies, 2,981 patients, 24 cognitive variables, searched through Jan 2009. Moderators (electrode placement, waveform and others) did not influence outcomes after 3 days. Conclusion: "Cognitive abnormalities associated with ECT are mainly limited to the first 3 days posttreatment… After 15 days, processing speed, working memory, anterograde memory, and some aspects of executive function improve beyond baseline levels." [GROUP; within-subject, no control arm, no RCT requirement] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20673880/).
  - Time bins: subacute 0–3 d, short-term 4–15 d, long-term >15 d. Variables were included only if they appeared in ≥3 studies. Evidence was lacking for several domains, and the CRD/DARE appraisal says caution is warranted because study validity was not assessed — [NCBI Books DARE summary](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK80031/).
  - It "did not examine retrograde autobiographical memory" — [FDA 2011 Executive Summary](https://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/41.-2011-FDA-Executive-Summary-on-ECT.pdf) (law-firm-hosted copy of an FDA document).
- **Semkovska et al. 2011, *J Psychiatr Res* 45:770–780.** 39 studies, 1,415 patients, brief-pulse only, 16 domains. Up to 3 days after the last treatment, unilateral ECT produced significantly smaller decreases than bitemporal in global cognition, delayed verbal recall and AM. Higher unilateral dose predicted larger acute decreases in verbal learning, delayed recall, visual recognition and semantic retrieval. **Beyond 3 days, no significant electrode-placement differences remained**, dose no longer predicted performance, and a longer interval since the last ECT predicted growing improvement. Significant publication bias was found for AM, favouring reports of larger percentage loss. [GROUP] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21109254/).
- **Landry et al. 2021, *J ECT* 37(2):119–127.** 91 studies (2000–2017), n=3,762, 11 domains. Time points: PO1 ≤24 h, PO2 1–28 days, PO3 >1 month. Verbal-fluency deficits correlated inversely with age, so younger adults had larger deficits. The authors call the MMSE's utility for tracking post-ECT change "extremely limited" and recommend measuring AM, verbal fluency and verbal memory. [GROUP] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009218/).
- **Guo et al. 2024, *Psychiatry Res* (doi 10.1016/j.psychres.2023.115611).** "Long-term" meta-analysis of 10 studies (5 RCTs, 5 quasi-experimental), n=868, searched to June 2023. Results: memory SMD +0.16 (−0.02 to 0.34), ns; attention +0.23 (ns); language −0.10 (ns); orientation −0.04 (ns); executive function and processing speed **+0.52 (0.29–0.74)** after excluding 3 studies; long-term learning **−0.37 (−0.55 to −0.18)**; global cognition on ECCA **−0.94 (−1.33 to −0.54)** versus MMSE/MoCA −0.09 (ns). [GROUP] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38101070/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165178123005619).
  - Quality flags: the ECCA subgroup came from a single RCT. Global cognition was pooled with a fixed-effect model despite I²=85%; under random effects the global SMD was −0.27 (−0.65 to 0.11), ns. The executive-function result became significant only after removing 3 studies — [ScienceDirect full text](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165178123005619).
- **Mathiassen, Semkovska, Lundsgaard, Gbyl & Videbech, *Br J Psychiatry* 2026;228(3):263–273, doi 10.1192/bjp.2025.2 (online May 2025).** 9 longitudinal studies with controls (432 ECT patients, 173 controls), searched to Jan 2024. Results:
  - Pooled post-ECT AM loss versus controls SMD 0.55 (0.35–0.75), I²=35%. Sensitivity range 0.51–0.60.
  - RUL small (0.32); mixed placement moderate; bilateral large (0.82).
  - **NNH = 5** for bilateral versus RUL: for every 5 patients given bilateral instead of RUL, one more has AM loss.
  - Higher age was associated with a *smaller* effect.
  - AM was stable between end of treatment and 6–12 months.
  - Limitations: mostly observational designs and small samples. [GROUP; moderate-quality meta of observational studies] — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/autobiographical-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/8F694868E2767C11F75DBA2C341C21AF); [PMC12916233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12916233).
- **Verspecht et al. 2026, *J Psychiatr Res* (KU Leuven; Obbels, Sienaert co-authors).** 12 studies, n=837, assessing both subjective and objective memory before and after ECT. Within 1 month: no change in immediate recall; significant decline in delayed recall and AM; significant improvement in subjective memory. Beyond 1 month: immediate and delayed recall significantly improved, AM significantly declined, and subjective memory improved. Substantial heterogeneity remained. [GROUP; effect-size magnitudes not in abstract] — [Lirias](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/4445896?limo=0).
- **Continuation/maintenance ECT meta-analysis (2022; studies from 2010 to June 2022).** 9 studies, ECT continued ≥2 months; numbers in the table above. Limitation: "Insufficient data resulted in imprecision" — [PubMed 35952935](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35952935/).
- **Kolshus, Jelovac & McLoughlin 2017, *Psychol Med* 47(3):518–530.** 7 RCTs, n=792, comparing bitemporal with high-dose RUL ECT; details under Q8 — [PDF](https://www.stpatricks.ie/media/1839/bitemporal-v-high-dose-right-unilateral-electroconvulsive-therapy-for-depression-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-randomized-controlled-trials.pdf). A corrigendum (Sept 2017) fixed switched group labels in Fig 3 (reorientation); results and conclusions unchanged — [Corrigendum record](https://exa.ai/library/publication/5vnkxcf9fyd).
- **Tor et al. 2015, *J Clin Psychiatry*.** Ultrabrief versus brief-pulse RUL; see Q8 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26213985/).
- Porter et al. (2020), summarising Semkovska's meta-analyses: in the subacute period (0–3 d) executive function and processing speed were impaired, attention was not; at 4–14 d most tests had improved and none were significantly below baseline. They warn that test-specific effects may reflect task properties rather than true domain sensitivity — [BJPsych Open 2020, PMC7191622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622).
- Sine-wave ECT, obsolete in high-income countries, causes significantly greater impairment, including reaction-time slowing still present at 6 months — [Porter 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622); [Sackeim 2007](https://www.nature.com/articles/1301180).

### Inferences
- The widely quoted message that "deficits are limited to the first 3 days" (Semkovska 2010) applies to anterograde and non-memory domains. It did not cover retrograde AM, and its "long-term" bin starts at >15 days, so it says little about >6 months.
- For AM, the 2010–2011 picture has been superseded or qualified by controlled data from 2016–2026 (EFFECT-Dep, Sackeim 2007/2008, Jelovac 2025, Mathiassen 2025). These show an ECT-attributable AM loss that persists to at least 3–6 months, possibly to 12 months, and is technique-dependent.
- Above-baseline performance at 1–6 months most plausibly reflects remission of depression-related cognitive impairment and practice effects, not a pro-cognitive action of ECT. Control groups also improve on some tests (see Q3).

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis pools objective non-AM cognition specifically beyond 12 months in adults of working age. Pooled >6-month data come mostly from older adults and maintenance-ECT cohorts.
- Domain-by-domain effect sizes from Semkovska 2010 (which variable had −1.10) and the cognitive SMDs for Tor 2015 were not retrievable from abstracts.
- Verspecht 2026 effect sizes were not available (abstract only).

## 2. Retrograde / autobiographical memory: prevalence, extent, permanence, type of memories, measurement

### Takeaway
Retrograde amnesia is the best-documented persistent cognitive effect of ECT.
- **Extent:** with brief-pulse bitemporal ECT, about 18–21% of baseline-recalled autobiographical items are lost *beyond* what depressed controls lose. With high-dose brief-pulse RUL ECT the figure is about 7–10%; with ultrabrief RUL it is smaller or not detectable.
- **Permanence:** loss is still measurable at 3–6 months in controlled studies and shows no recovery to 6–12 months.
- **Type of memory:** it concentrates on events closest to the treatment (roughly the months before and during the course), on impersonal and public information more than personal memories, and on less salient events. Very remote and highly salient memories are relatively spared.
- **Individual level:** in a large community sample (mixed techniques, 2000s), about 12% of patients had marked AM loss that persisted to 6 months, and only bilateral ECT distinguished them.

### Cited Findings

**Prevalence and extent**
- **Sackeim et al. 2007, *Neuropsychopharmacology* 32:244–254 (CORE-era community study).** Prospective naturalistic study at 7 New York facilities; 751 referred, 347 assessed. AMI-SF, mMMS and Buschke delayed recall were measured shortly after ECT and at 6 months. Findings:
  - "Adverse cognitive effects were detected 6 months following the acute treatment course."
  - Bilateral ECT caused "more severe and persisting retrograde amnesia than right unilateral ECT."
  - Sine wave slowed reaction time both immediately and at 6 months.
  - Age, lower premorbid IQ and female sex predicted greater deficits.
  - [GROUP+INDIV; observational] — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/1301180).
  - **[INDIV] Of 306 patients classified, 38 (12.4%) met a priori criteria for "marked and persistent retrograde amnesia"** (AMI-SF decrease ≥2.0 SD at both the post-ECT and 6-month assessments). "Only receipt of BL treatment distinguished the group with marked and persistent retrograde amnesia" — [full text PDF](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf).
  - **[GROUP]** In patients treated only with bilateral ECT, mean AMI-SF decrement was **3.4× (post-ECT) and 2.8× (6 months)** the forgetting seen in healthy comparison groups. At 6 months, the number of bilateral treatments still predicted retrograde amnesia (slope −0.06, p=0.002); RUL and bifrontal did not — [full text PDF](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf).
- **Sackeim et al. 2008, *Brain Stimul* 1:71–83.** RCT, n=90: RUL 6× seizure threshold versus bilateral 2.5×, each at 0.3 ms or 1.5 ms pulse width; 118-item AMI.
  - At 6 months, patients given brief-pulse bilateral ECT "could not recall any information or gave inconsistent responses to more than 22% of the questions they answered at baseline." Error rates were **10% (brief-pulse RUL), 6% (ultrabrief bilateral), 6% (ultrabrief RUL)**.
  - Pulse-width effects on retrograde amnesia "persisted at least six months."
  - More treatments and older age predicted greater amnesia at 2 and 6 months. [GROUP; RCT] — [PMC2742986](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742986/).
- **Jelovac et al. 2025, *BJPsych Open* 11(3):e81 (McLoughlin group, Dublin).** Propensity-score comparison of 210 patients: brief-pulse (1.0 ms) bitemporal 1.5× ST, high-dose RUL 6× ST, and ECT-naive depressed inpatient controls. AMI-SF loss attributable to ECT (ATT):
  - RUL: **−7.6% at end of treatment and −9.4% at 3 months**.
  - Bitemporal: **−18.7% and −21.0%**.
  - Across sensitivity methods: 7–10% (RUL) and 18–21% (bitemporal).
  - The difference was "unattenuated" at 3 months, i.e. persisting.
  - **Depressed controls themselves lost 24–25% AMI-SF consistency over the same interval.** Within-group drops in uncontrolled studies therefore overstate ECT-specific amnesia.
  - Comparable RUL figures in an RCT of younger bipolar patients: 7.9% at end of treatment and **8.0% at 6 months**. [GROUP; observational, propensity-adjusted] — [PMC12052575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12052575/).
- **EFFECT-Dep RCT (Semkovska et al. 2016, *Am J Psychiatry* 173:408–417).** n=138, twice-weekly ECT, bitemporal 1.5× versus RUL 6×. AMI-SF consistency was lower with bitemporal at end of treatment (OR 0.66, 0.51–0.85), at 3 months (OR 0.59, 0.45–0.78) and at **6 months (OR 0.59, 0.45–0.79)**. "Bitemporal ECT was associated with a lower percent recall of autobiographical information… that persisted for 6 months." [GROUP; RCT; individual distributions in supplement Table S3, not retrieved] — [AJP](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15030372).
- **Mathiassen et al. meta-analysis:** pooled SMD 0.55 overall, RUL 0.32, bilateral 0.82, NNH 5. "The autobiographical memory loss remains stable between end-of-treatment and long-term follow-up (6–12 months), indicating that lost memories are not regained." The authors frame permanence as a "tentative conclusion" — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/autobiographical-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/8F694868E2767C11F75DBA2C341C21AF).
- **Porter et al. 2020 (BJPsych Open):**
  - AM loss "exceeds the normal decay of memories over time in healthy matched participants."
  - "In studies where it is objectively measured for at least 1 year this loss has been found to persist. Whether it continues for longer than 1 year is not clear from objective studies, but some patients report that it does and it seems unlikely that lost memories could return spontaneously after this point."
  - Individual-level identification of severe loss "has rarely been done"; where done, females and bilateral ECT were at higher risk — [PMC7191622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622).

**Type and timing of lost memories**
- **Lisanby et al. 2000, *Arch Gen Psychiatry* 57:581–590.** RCT, n=55, RUL versus bilateral at low and high dose; Personal and Impersonal Memory Test. Shortly after ECT, deficits were greater for impersonal than personal events. Bilateral ECT caused more amnesia, especially for impersonal memories, independent of dose and clinical outcome. At 2 months, retrograde amnesia was reduced, but deficits persisted for impersonal events and for details of recent impersonal events. Amnestic effects are greatest and most persistent "for knowledge about the world (impersonal memory)… for recent compared with distinctly remote events, and for less salient events" — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10839336); [full text](http://www.ectresources.org/ECTscience/Lisanby_et_al__2000__Memory_loss___Public_Events_more_than_Autobiographical___Devanand_coauthor_.pdf).
- **Fraser, O'Carroll & Ebmeier 2008, *J ECT* 24:10–17.** Systematic review of 15 studies, n=1,243. ECT "predominantly affects memory of prior personal events that are near the treatment (within 6 months)." Objective measures found loss "relatively short term (<6 months)", whereas subjective accounts reported persistence >6 months. Three studies described persistent effects up to several years. [GROUP; pre-2008 small studies] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18379329/); [DARE summary](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK76502/).
  - Superseded in part: the "<6 months objective" conclusion is contradicted by later controlled data (Sackeim 2007/2008, EFFECT-Dep, Mathiassen 2025).
- Squire's 3-year follow-up of bilateral ECT (as summarised by McElhiney et al. 1995) reported subjective "gaps in memory for events that occurred on average 6 months before and 2 months after bilateral (BL) ECT." McElhiney 1995 also found that 2-month persistent deficits were related to a second ECT course and to bilateral ECT, not to clinical improvement or the emotional valence of memories — [McElhiney 1995 PDF](https://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/26.-McElhiney-et-al.1995-Autobiographical-memory-and-mood-Effects-of-ECT.pdf) (law-firm-hosted copy).
- **APA Task Force 1978** (historical, bilateral/older techniques) concluded:
  - Very remote memories "appear to recover fully"
  - "Memories acquired during the days prior to a course of ECT may be permanently lost"
  - "Extensive ECT (e.g., more than 50 treatments) may lead to long-lasting or permanent impairment… but a definitive conclusion is not yet possible"
  - — [1978 report PDF](https://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/1978-APA-Task-Force-Report-on-ECT_Full-version.pdf) (law-firm-hosted; historical only).
- **APA Task Force 2001** (quoted in a peer-reviewed ethics paper): "Retrograde amnesia occurs to some extent in almost all ECT recipients… In some patients the recovery from retrograde amnesia will be incomplete, and evidence has shown that ECT can result in persistent or permanent memory loss" — [PMC5811419](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5811419) (secondary quotation).
- **Sackeim 2000** (*J ECT* 16:87–96, "Memory and ECT: from polarization to reconciliation") is quoted as stating "Virtually all patients experience some degree of persistent and, likely, permanent retrograde amnesia", substantially greater with bilateral than RUL — [NY State Assembly report](https://nyassembly.gov/comm/Mental/20020416) (secondary quotation; primary [PubMed 10868319](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10868319) abstract not retrieved). Sackeim also documented rare cases of dense retrograde amnesia extending years back. A first-person account (Donahue 2000, *J ECT*) describes losing major life events from 8–9 years before treatment — [Current Psychiatry letters](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/0510CP_Letters.pdf) (case report level).
- **Predictor:** Sobin et al. 1995 (*Am J Psychiatry*) found that time to reorientation after each treatment correlated with AM loss at 2 months. The correlation was driven by a small group taking >30 minutes to reorient; Porter et al. recommend recording reorientation time and flagging >30 min — [Porter 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622).

**Measurement**
- The CUAMI / AMI-SF score is the percentage of items answered at baseline that are answered consistently later. It measures consistency, not absolute loss, and is "irrespective of how good/poor baseline performance was" — [Kolshus 2017](https://www.stpatricks.ie/media/1839/bitemporal-v-high-dose-right-unilateral-electroconvulsive-therapy-for-depression-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-randomized-controlled-trials.pdf).
- Semkovska & McLoughlin 2013 (*J ECT* 29:127–133): normal AM recall loses consistency over time, and depression itself reduces specific AM recall, possibly persisting into euthymia. Few studies separate ECT effects from normal and mood-related forgetting. This "prevents us at present from objectively quantifying the nature and extent" of ECT-associated retrograde amnesia — [SDU portal](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/measuring-retrograde-autobiographical-amnesia-following-electroco/).
- Jelovac 2025 quantified the issue: depressed controls lost 24–25% consistency over about 3 months, so uncontrolled within-group AMI-SF drops should not be read as ECT amnesia — [PMC12052575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12052575/).
- Conflicting secondary summary: a 2025 retrospective paper states that objectively measured AM impairment shows "nearly complete resolution between 1 month and 6 months" while patients report persistent amnesia beyond that — [Riessland 2025, PMC13064711](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13064711). This conflicts with Mathiassen 2025, Jelovac 2025 and EFFECT-Dep.

### Inferences
- **Well established:** ECT causes measurable retrograde AM loss beyond normal and depression-related forgetting. It is larger with bilateral than RUL (NNH 5), larger with brief than ultrabrief pulse, and grows with the number of treatments. Controlled studies show no meaningful recovery between end of course and 3–6 months, so lost items are probably permanently lost.
- **Uncertain:** the absolute size of the loss in *individual* patients receiving modern ultrabrief RUL ECT. In Sackeim 2008, ultrabrief RUL error rates at 6 months (6%) were close to typical forgetting levels, and the group showed no significant impairment versus its own baseline. Also uncertain: whether loss ever extends years back with modern techniques (case-level evidence only); and the functional significance of a 7–10% consistency loss on a 30-item interview.
- For a patient planning medical school, the content at risk appears to be mainly event and episode memory from the months around treatment, plus impersonal and news facts from that period. Lisanby's finding that public and impersonal knowledge is more vulnerable could plausibly extend to material studied shortly before or during a course, but no study directly tests retention of academic coursework.

### Gaps
- No controlled objective AM data beyond 12 months.
- No study found that measures AM loss with ultrabrief RUL against depressed controls at ≥6 months.
- Individual distributions from EFFECT-Dep (Table S3) and the Sackeim 2007 breakdown of the 38 high-loss patients by technique were not retrieved.
- No data on loss of studied academic or professional knowledge (as opposed to autobiographical or public events).

## 3. Anterograde memory and executive function: full recovery? Improvement above baseline?

### Takeaway
At group level, anterograde verbal memory (especially delayed recall), verbal fluency and processing speed dip acutely (d ≈ −0.25 to −0.3 in a 634-patient pooled cohort; larger with brief-pulse or bilateral ECT). They recover by about 2–4 weeks and are at or above baseline at 3–6 months. Improvement beyond baseline appears consistently from about 1 month and is best explained by remission of depression plus practice effects. Evidence for a lasting *anterograde* deficit at 6 months is limited to sine-wave ECT (reaction time) and to subsets of individual patients (Q4).

### Cited Findings
- **Semkovska 2010:** after 15 days, processing speed, working memory, anterograde memory and some executive functions improved beyond baseline (ES +0.35 to +0.75 in 57% of variables). [GROUP] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20673880/).
- **Semkovska 2011:** a longer interval after ECT "predicted growing improvement in some variables"; no placement differences after 3 days. [GROUP] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21109254/).
- **Landry 2021:** at 1–28 days, small-to-medium declines in verbal fluency and verbal memory; after more than 1 month, improvement on almost all domains including verbal fluency and verbal memory. [GROUP] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009218/).
- **Loef et al. 2024, *J Affect Disord* (Dutch ECT Consortium).** Pooled cohort of 634 patients with a major depressive episode, 5 sites, 2001–2020, extensive battery, mixed models; up to 6 months.
  - Directly after ECT, only animal fluency (d −0.25), letter fluency (d −0.29) and verbal recall (d −0.26) declined significantly.
  - "During three and six months of follow-up, cognitive performance across all domains significantly improved, even outperforming baseline levels."
  - Some measures declined between 3 and 6 months, possibly linked to relapse, which runs at about 50% by 6 months.
  - Missing data were substantial at 6 months. [GROUP; observational multicentre] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165032724000594); [PDF](https://dutchectconsortium.nl/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1-s2.0-S0165032724000594-main.pdf).
- **Hebbrecht et al. 2022, *J Psychiatr Res* 156:132–140.** n=73; MoCA, HVLT-R, SDST, AMI-C; up to 6 months. Group level: temporary deterioration in processing speed, verbal memory (learning and delayed recall) and retrograde personal-semantic memory during and directly after the course. "All cognitive task scores returned to baseline values at long-term follow-up." Latent-class analysis found 3 parallel trajectories that differed mainly in pre-ECT level. [GROUP+INDIV] — [PDF](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/185704552/Cognitive-trajectories-during-and-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-in-patients-with-mde.pdf).
- **Mohn & Rund 2016, *Front Psychiatry*.** n=31, MATRICS battery, baseline / 6 weeks / 6 months.
  - Significant improvement at 6 months in speed of processing (TMT-A 48.7→37.0 s; symbol coding 40.5→46.4), fluency (21.3→24.5), reasoning (Mazes 13.5→16.1) and attention (CPT-IP 2.5→2.8).
  - Working memory, verbal and visual learning, and social cognition unchanged.
  - "At no point was there any cognitive deterioration compared to baseline." [GROUP; small, no control group] — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2016.00200/full).
- **Vasavada et al. 2017.** n=44 (32 at 6 months); 61% RUL, the rest mixed or bitemporal; 33 healthy controls.
  - Acute decline in attention and verbal memory after 2 sessions, back to baseline by the end of the index course.
  - Processing speed improved through 6 months, but **controls also improved in processing speed (practice effects)**.
  - Episodic memory was not measured. [GROUP; controlled for practice] — [PMC5705261](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5705261/).
- **Verspecht 2026 meta-analysis:** beyond 1 month, immediate and delayed recall *significantly improved* — [Lirias](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/4445896?limo=0).
- **Guo 2024 meta-analysis:** long-term executive function and processing speed SMD +0.52 (improvement), but "long-term learning" SMD −0.37 (worse); quality caveats in Q1 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38101070/).
- **ELEKT-D RCT (Anand et al. 2023, *NEJM* 388:2315).** 403 randomised; ECT three times weekly versus IV ketamine for 3 weeks.
  - HVLT-R delayed-recall T-score change: **−9.7 ± 1.2 with ECT versus −0.9 ± 1.1 with ketamine** (difference 8.8, 95% CI 5.7–11.9). This is an end-of-course (acute) measurement, "with gradual recovery during follow-up."
  - Among ECT responders, patient-rated memory (GSE-My) remained lower than with ketamine through the 6-month follow-up. [GROUP; RCT, open-label] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37224232/); [full text](https://www.nrxpharma.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Anand-et-al-ketamine-vs-ECT-2.pdf).
- **Sackeim 2007:** sine-wave stimulation produced pronounced reaction-time slowing "both immediately and 6 months following ECT" — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/1301180).
- **Porter 2020:** even though group data show recovery within 14 days, "patients may be attempting to return to their roles in this period"; "recovery of new learning and executive function is variable" in the weeks after ECT, with possible impact on driving and work — [PMC7191622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622).

### Inferences
- **Well established:** at group level, anterograde memory and executive deficits are transient and resolve within weeks with brief-pulse or ultrabrief, RUL or bilateral techniques. The above-baseline gains from 1 to 6 months are real but mostly reflect lifted depression and practice. They should not be read as ECT enhancing cognition, and they do not offset AM loss.
- For someone planning an intensive academic return, the relevant window is the first 2–4 weeks after the last treatment (and the duration of any continuation ECT). Verbal recall and fluency are the most affected domains during that window.

### Gaps
- Few studies use alternate test forms, healthy or depressed control arms and ≥6-month follow-up together, so practice effects and depression-recovery effects are not cleanly separated.
- No data on high-demand real-world learning (for example exam performance) beyond small adolescent series (Q7).

## 4. Individual-level rates of persistent ("lasting") impairment

### Takeaway
Group means hide heterogeneity. Using reliable-change methods, roughly **5–23% of patients score reliably worse than baseline on a given test at 6 months**. About **8% decline on a global screen (MoCA)** and about **12% decline on ≥2 tests**. A similar or larger share improve reliably (14–60% depending on test). For retrograde AM specifically, about **12% had marked loss persisting to 6 months** in a mixed-technique community sample, concentrated in bilateral ECT. None of these individual-level studies had an ECT-naive depressed control arm, so the share due to ECT rather than test noise, relapse or illness course is unknown.

### Cited Findings
- **Sackeim 2007 (community, mixed techniques):** 38/306 (12.4%) had marked and persistent retrograde amnesia (≥2 SD AMI-SF drop at post-ECT *and* 6 months). Bilateral ECT was the only distinguishing factor. [INDIV] — [full text PDF](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf).
- **Hebbrecht et al. 2020 (MoCA; 90% reliable-change index (RCI) threshold, alternate MoCA forms).** n≈65 assessed at end of course, ≈51 at 6 months; major depressive episode. [INDIV; uncontrolled] — [Exa library record](https://exa.ai/library/publication/pb3vj65rbms).
  - **1 week post-ECT:** 26% (17) reliably worse, 12% improved, 62% stable.
  - **6 months:** **8% (4) reliably worse**, 18% (9) improved, 75% stable.
  - Earlier time points: worse 21.3% (during ECT), 26.2% (1 week), 5.3% (3 months), 7.8% (6 months).
  - "Deteriorators" at 1 week had *higher* baseline MoCA.
- **Hebbrecht et al. 2022 (n=73):** directly after ECT, 20–26% deteriorated on MoCA or HVLT-R total learning, 7–11% improved, 63–73% stable. "At T4 [6 months], the percentages of improvers for all cognitive test scores were consistently higher than the percentages of deteriorators" [INDIV] — [PDF](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/185704552/Cognitive-trajectories-during-and-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-in-patients-with-mde.pdf).
- **Loef et al. 2024 (n=634; RCI ±1.645).** Share reliably deteriorated per measure: **5–17% directly after ECT, 2–9% at 3 months, 4–23% at 6 months**. Share improved: 1–30%, 0–47% and 0–60% respectively. The 6-month figures are affected by substantial missing data and about 50% relapse. [INDIV] — [PDF](https://dutchectconsortium.nl/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1-s2.0-S0165032724000594-main.pdf).
- **Obbels et al. 2018, *Acta Psychiatr Scand* 138:223–231 (MODECT; n=110, aged ≥55).** No group-level change at 6 months on any test. **12% (95% CI 5.8–22%) showed significant decline on ≥2 tests**; 14% (6.7–24%) significant improvement on ≥2 tests. [INDIV; older adults] — [Exa library record](https://exa.ai/library/publication/4gn944ts42d); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acps.12942).
- **Obbels et al. 2021, *Acta Psychiatr Scand* 143:141–150 (n=41, aged ≥55).** Assessments at 6 months and at 1, 2, 3 and 4 years: no statistically significant group-level change from pre-ECT to 4 years, but "clinically important differences on an individual level", both decline and improvement. [GROUP+INDIV; small, older] — [Amsterdam UMC](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/en/publications/neurocognitive-functioning-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-in-lat/).
- **Sackeim 2008 RCT:** 71% of all patients rated ECT's global effect on memory as negative, 26% no effect, 2% positive. Ultrabrief RUL patients gave the least negative ratings. [INDIV, subjective] — [PMC2742986](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742986/).
- **Porter 2020:** identification of individuals with especially severe AM loss "has rarely been done"; risk is higher in females and with bilateral ECT — [PMC7191622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622).

### Inferences
- **Best available individual-level estimate:**
  - With modern techniques, most patients (≈75–80%) are cognitively stable versus baseline at 6 months on standard tests; a similar or larger fraction improve rather than decline.
  - About 5–12% show reliable decline on global or multi-test criteria.
  - About 12% showed marked persistent AM loss in a 2000s community sample with substantial bilateral and some sine-wave use; this is likely lower with ultrabrief RUL (Sackeim 2008; Mathiassen NNH 5).
- **These are upper bounds for ECT-attributable lasting harm, not point estimates.** Under the 90% RCI used, about 5% per test would fall below the threshold by chance. With 10 or more tests, "≥2 declines" can occur by chance, and relapse also lowers scores. No study had a matched non-ECT depressed comparison for individual-level rates.

### Gaps
- No study found reports individual-level reliable change at ≥6 months with ECT-naive depressed controls, or specifically in young, high-IQ patients receiving ultrabrief RUL.
- Sackeim 2007's 12.4% has not been replicated with modern ultrabrief RUL.
- EFFECT-Dep individual AMI-SF distributions were not retrieved.

## 5. Long-term cohorts (1–10+ years), dementia registries, cumulative exposure

### Takeaway
Registry studies totalling about 287,000 patients, with follow-ups of about 5 to 34 years, show **no increased dementia risk** after ECT; risk is lower among older adults. Repeated courses (up to 186 sessions) did not produce cumulative deficits on screening tests. Small prospective cohorts followed for 2–4 years show stable group-level cognition. Dementia is a coarse endpoint, however, and cannot detect subtle deficits in high-level cognition.

### Cited Findings
- **Osler et al. 2018, *Lancet Psychiatry* 5:348–356 (Denmark).**
  - Population: 168,015 patients aged ≥10 with first hospital contact for an affective disorder, 2005–2015; 5,901 (3.5%) had ECT. Median follow-up 4.9 years.
  - Crude dementia: 3.6% with ECT versus 3.1% without (70.4 versus 59.2 per 10,000 person-years).
  - **Age <50: HR 1.51 (0.67–3.46), ns.** Age 50–69: 1.15 (0.91–1.47). Age ≥70: 0.68 (0.58–0.80), attenuated to 0.77 (0.59–1.00) with propensity matching.
  - Competing-risk subdistribution HR 0.98 (0.76–1.26). [GROUP; registry] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29523431/).
  - Patients with ≥10 ECT sessions were also not at higher risk — [Psychiatric News](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.pn.2018.4b23).
- **Hjerrild et al. 2021 (Aarhus, Denmark).** 1,089 ECT inpatients (1982–2000) versus 3,011 non-ECT inpatients, follow-up up to 34 years. Cumulative dementia incidence 13.45% (ECT) versus 10.53% (non-ECT). Adjusted HR for non-ECT versus ECT 0.73 (0.52–1.04), ns. No age-stratum differences — [Exa library record](https://exa.ai/library/publication/30t2n8kgy76).
- **Chu et al. 2018, *Front Psychiatry* 9:397 (Taiwan NHIRD).** 994 ECT versus 2,982 controls (46.7% schizophrenia), about 10-year follow-up. Dementia 4.53% versus 5.0%; adjusted HR 0.633 (0.448–1.895), ns. Prolonged seizures, post-ECT delirium and stroke within the ECT cohort were associated with higher dementia risk — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2018.00397/pdf).
- **Kirov et al. 2025, *Acta Psychiatr Scand* (Wales).** 110,774 people aged 35–65 hospitalised with affective disorders; 1,010 had ECT; mean follow-up 24.5 years. Dementia 15.4% versus 13.1%. Adjusted HR **0.888 (0.757–1.044)**; Fine-Gray 1.104 (0.945–1.289). Mortality HR 0.81 — [PMC12404881](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12404881/).
- **Kassar et al. 2025 (GRADE-assessed meta-analysis, *Int Psychogeriatr*).** 6 cohorts, 286,995 patients (9,124 ECT), median follow-up 4.9–16.9 years. Pooled HR **0.98 (0.79–1.21)**, I²=67%, very low certainty. Adults >50: HR 0.78 (0.69–0.88), moderate certainty — [PubMed 41176476](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41176476/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1041610225003977).
- **Kirov et al. 2016, *Br J Psychiatry* 208:266–270 (Cardiff).** 10 years of clinical data, 199 patients, 498 assessments after a mean of 15.3 sessions (range 0–186). "The total number of previous ECT sessions had no effect on cognitive performance"; age, current depression severity and days since last ECT mattered. [GROUP; clinical screening data] — [Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/bjps/pdf/10.1192/bjp.bp.114.158261~evaluation-of-cumulative-cognitive-deficits-from).
- **Continuation/maintenance ECT meta-analysis 2022:** no detriment at 6 and 12 months (numbers in Q1) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35952935/).
- **Obbels 2021:** 4-year stable group trajectory in late-life depression (n=41) — [Amsterdam UMC](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/en/publications/neurocognitive-functioning-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-in-lat/).
- **Adolescent follow-ups** (Cohen 2000 at 3.5 years; Taieb 2002 at 5.2 years; de la Serna 2011 at 2 years): see Q7.
- **Swedish registry (Q-ECT) work** retrieved here concerns subjective memory, not dementia (Brus 2017; Sigström 2020; Tornhamre 2025; see Q6). A Swedish register study of ECT in the oldest-old exists (Arnison 2025, *Am J Geriatr Psychiatry*), but its cognitive results were not retrieved — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1064748125003811).

### Inferences
- **Well established:** ECT does not raise later dementia risk, and repeated or maintenance courses do not cause cumulative global decline.
- **Not addressed by these data:** subtle, domain-specific or high-ceiling deficits relevant to a medical student, and retrograde AM. The under-50 Danish HR (1.51, CI 0.67–3.46) is imprecise rather than reassuring or alarming.

### Gaps
- No prospective study with ≥1-year objective cognitive follow-up in young adults of working age with major depression (only adolescent case series and older-adult cohorts).
- No registry outcome capturing educational attainment or occupational performance after ECT.
- No Swedish dementia-registry result for ECT was found.

## 6. Subjective (patient-reported) memory complaints versus objective tests

### Takeaway
How many patients report lasting memory problems depends heavily on how the question is asked:
- **≈16–26%** when worsening is defined as a 2-point drop on a rated memory item from before to after ECT (Swedish register; 25.2% at 6 months in n=1,498).
- **≈48%** pooled prevalence of any complaint (meta-analysis of 72 studies).
- **≈55%** when patients are asked retrospectively whether ECT harmed their memory; Rose 2003's historical range was **29–55%**.

Complaints track residual depression (r≈0.46 between change in mood and change in complaints) much more than anterograde test scores. They do not fade with time once present. Where they correspond to anything objective, it appears to be autobiographical memory loss rather than anterograde test scores.

### Cited Findings
- **Rose et al. 2003, *BMJ* 326:1363.** Descriptive systematic review: 26 clinician-led and 9 patient-led or collaborative studies; 7 met criteria for memory loss. "At least one third of patients reported persistent memory loss." [Mostly 1980–2000 studies, heterogeneous, older techniques] — [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/326/7403/1363).
  - The range is commonly cited as 29–55%. Later authors note these studies "may be outdated owing to changes in ECT techniques" and could not separate acute from persistent effects — [Sigström 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/longterm-subjective-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy/B11433F4BE1F19DBA4C60B887DA63446).
- **Semkovska, Knittle, Leahy & Rasmussen 2023, *Aust N Z J Psychiatry* 57(1):21–33.** 72 studies, n=5,699 (brief pulse), searched 1972 to Aug 2020.
  - Weighted mean prevalence of patients with cognitive complaints after ECT: **48.1% (42.3–53.9%)**.
  - Pre-post change depended on the instrument: Squire Self-Rating Scale of Memory **g = +0.81 (0.59–1.03), improvement**; 1-item memory scales **g = −0.48 (−0.70 to −0.26), worsening**; overall g = +0.18.
  - Correlation between decrease in depression and decrease in complaints: **r = 0.46 (0.39–0.53)**, explaining about 21% of variance (22 studies, n=2,010).
  - Unlike objective effects, complaints "did not decrease with the passage of time following the end of treatment" (1 day to 2 years).
  - Objective improvement did not predict complaints, except via the SRSM.
  - Strongest predictors of better subjective cognition: older age and greater mood improvement. [GROUP] — [doi.org/10.1177/00048674221089231](https://doi.org/10.1177/00048674221089231).
- **Brus et al. 2017, *J ECT* 33:96–103 (Swedish Q-ECT, n=1,212).** Subjective memory worsening (≥2-point CPRS memory-item worsening within 1 week) in **26%**.
  - Women 31% versus men 18%; age **18–39: 32%** versus ≥65: 22%.
  - Higher if no memory complaints before ECT (39%); lower if in remission.
  - **Brief versus ultrabrief pulse OR 1.61 (1.05–2.47).** [INDIV; registry] — [PDF](http://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/yct50139-96.103.pdf).
- **Sigström, Nordenskjöld, Juréus, Clements, Joas, Pålsson & Landén 2020, *BJPsych Open* 6(2):e26.** n=277 analysed of 535; CPRS memory item before ECT and a median of 73 days after.
  - Subjective memory **worsened in 16.2%, unchanged in 52.3%, improved in 31.4%**. Only **7.9%** had worsening plus clinically significant impairment.
  - But when asked retrospectively, **54.6% reported a negative effect** of ECT on memory.
  - Worsening was associated with negative expectations before ECT, younger age and shorter follow-up. [INDIV] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148217/); [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/longterm-subjective-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy/B11433F4BE1F19DBA4C60B887DA63446).
- **Tornhamre, Hammar, Nordanskog & Nordenskjöld 2025, *J Affect Disord* 372:324–332 (Q-ECT, n=1,498).** Long-term subjective memory worsening at 6 months in **25.2%**. Associated with more depressive symptoms and lower education; **"No association could be found related to ECT technical factors"** or number of sessions. Age and comorbidity effects were mediated by depressive symptoms. [INDIV; registry] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S016503272402007X).
- **Verspecht 2026:** subjective memory improved both within and beyond 1 month while AM declined. At study level, larger AM decline went with smaller subjective improvement immediately after ECT, but not beyond 1 month; no such link with verbal learning. "Study level associations do not necessarily imply patient level associations" — [Lirias](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/4445896?limo=0).
- **Fraser 2008:** objective AM loss was relatively short-term (<6 months) whereas subjective amnesia was reported as more persistent (>6 months) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18379329/).
- **Mohn & Rund 2016:** Everyday Memory Questionnaire did not correlate with MATRICS tests but did correlate with MADRS (r=0.49) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2016.00200/full).
- **EFFECT-Dep:** fewer subjective cognitive side effects with high-dose RUL than bitemporal at end of treatment (p=0.02) and at 6 months (p=0.025) — [AJP](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15030372).
- **ELEKT-D:** patient-rated memory (GSE-My) remained lower in ECT responders than ketamine responders through 6 months, while the SMCQ showed gradual recovery — [full text](https://www.nrxpharma.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Anand-et-al-ketamine-vs-ECT-2.pdf).
- **Cohen 2000 (adolescents):** 6/10 reported memory loss immediately after ECT; 1/10 complained at a mean 3.5-year follow-up — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10698827/).

### Inferences
- **Well established:** subjective complaints are common (about a quarter to a half, depending on definition), persist once present, and are driven substantially by residual depression. Anterograde test scores do not explain them.
- **Plausible but not established:** a residue of complaints reflects true autobiographical gaps that standard batteries do not measure. This is supported by the study-level Verspecht link, the persistence of AM loss in controlled studies, and Fraser's objective/subjective split.
- Persistent subjective complaints after ECT are more likely when depression does not remit. So achieving and maintaining remission is itself a major determinant of whether a patient perceives lasting cognitive harm.

### Gaps
- Few patient-level studies correlate long-term subjective complaints with long-term AM consistency measured against controls.
- The Rose 2003 constituent studies are pre-2000 and used older techniques; no equivalent patient-led review covering modern ultrabrief RUL practice was found.

## 7. Younger adults, high-functioning or high-IQ patients, students, professionals

### Takeaway
No study specifically examines physicians, medical students or high-IQ professionals after ECT. Indirect evidence points two ways:
- **Reassuring:** small adolescent series followed 2–5 years show no measurable cognitive or school-achievement deficit versus matched psychiatric controls. In 424 patients aged 16–30, the acute MoCA drop (−1.1) was not age-dependent. Lower (not higher) premorbid IQ predicted greater deficits in the largest community study.
- **Cautionary:** several studies find that **younger age and higher pre-ECT cognitive function predict larger *measurable* acute declines and more subjective memory worsening**, probably because there is more to lose and less ceiling. Older age also predicted a smaller AM effect in the 2025 meta-analysis.

### Cited Findings
- **Luccarelli et al. (PMC8669295; retrospective, single centre).** 424 patients aged 16–30 (2011–2020): MoCA 26.7 ± 2.6 → 25.6 ± 3.0 at treatment #10 (Δ −1.1), tested about 48–72 h after the previous session. Age did not predict MoCA change; only baseline MoCA did. The authors note Δ1.1 is below published MoCA minimal clinically important differences (1.22–2.15, from stroke populations). No long-term follow-up. [GROUP] — [PMC8669295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8669295/).
- **Cohen et al. 2000, *Am J Psychiatry* 157:460–462.** 10 adolescents given **bilateral** ECT for severe mood disorder, assessed a mean 3.5 years (SD 1.7) later against 10 matched psychiatric controls. All cognitive scores (including CVLT) were similar to controls and to community norms; 1/10 had a subjective complaint at follow-up. [GROUP; very small] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10698827/).
- **Taieb et al. 2002, *Eur Psychiatry*.** 11 adolescents given bilateral ECT versus 10 matched controls, mean 5.2 years (range 2–9). No difference in social functioning or **school achievement**; impact on schooling was related to illness severity, not ECT. [Very small] — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-psychiatry/article/abs/clinical-relevance-of-electroconvulsive-therapy-ect-in-adolescents-with-severe-mood-disorder-evidence-from-a-followup-study/96CB568A78697065C2748386EAD184DE).
- **de la Serna et al. 2011 (adolescent schizophrenia spectrum, n=9 versus 9).** No group difference in cognitive change at 2 years — [Exa library record](https://exa.ai/library/publication/tlym55ldty2).
- **Loef 2024 (n=634):** "No other pre-treatment factor than a younger age predicted a larger deterioration in cognitive performance shortly after ECT" (verbal fluency), attributed to "more intact cognitive functioning prior to ECT". R² was only 0.02–0.08 — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165032724000594).
- **Hebbrecht 2020:** patients who reliably deteriorated on MoCA 1 week post-ECT had higher baseline MoCA (F=9.75, p<0.001) — [Exa library record](https://exa.ai/library/publication/pb3vj65rbms).
- **Landry 2021:** verbal-fluency deficits correlated inversely with age, so younger adults showed greater deficits (short term) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009218/).
- **Brus 2017:** subjective memory worsening 32% at age 18–39 versus 22% at ≥65; 31% in women versus 18% in men — [PDF](http://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/yct50139-96.103.pdf).
- **Sigström 2020:** younger age associated with subjective memory worsening — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148217/).
- **Mathiassen 2025:** higher age associated with *smaller* AM loss, by implication larger in younger patients; the authors say this "warrants further research" — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/autobiographical-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/8F694868E2767C11F75DBA2C341C21AF).
- **Sackeim 2007:** "Advancing age, lower premorbid intellectual function, and female gender were associated with greater cognitive deficits" — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/1301180).
- **Jelovac 2025:** a comparable RUL AM loss (7.9% end of treatment, 8.0% at 6 months) was seen in an RCT of "all bipolar and younger" patients — [PMC12052575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12052575/).
- A 2025 PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis of cognitive trajectories after ECT in adolescents with major depression is in progress; no results yet — [Zha et al. 2025 protocol](https://exa.ai/library/publication/x2p9pcxnjhp).
- **Return-to-work guidance:** Porter 2020 notes deficits in new learning and executive function in the weeks after ECT "may have an impact on activities such as driving and work". Temporary impairment "may set up a cycle of difficulty in functioning, negative appraisal of performance and avoidance of cognitive activity" — [PMC7191622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622).

### Inferences
- The apparent conflict between "younger and higher-functioning patients show more acute decline" and "lower premorbid IQ predicts greater deficits" is plausibly explained by ceiling and measurement. High-baseline patients have more room to show a reliable drop on screening tests. Their long-term group trajectories (Hebbrecht 2022 latent classes) run parallel to others, differing mainly in starting level.
- For a young, high-functioning patient who is prone to side effects, the evidence suggests larger acute or subacute drops and a higher chance of noticing and reporting memory worsening. There is no evidence of worse long-term anterograde outcomes. AM loss may be relatively larger at younger ages; this rests on a single meta-regression.

### Gaps
- **No data** on physicians, medical or graduate students, or professionals after ECT, and no studies of academic performance (grades, exams) in adults.
- Only anecdotal or first-person accounts exist (e.g., Donahue 2000, a case of dense retrograde amnesia — [letters](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/Document/September-2017/0510CP_Letters.pdf)). No systematic case series of return to high-level cognitive work was found.
- No study stratifies outcomes by premorbid IQ in the high range.

## 8. Traditional bilateral brief-pulse versus modern RUL ultrabrief: numbers by time point (brief; technique covered by another researcher)

### Takeaway
Electrode placement and pulse width are the largest modifiable determinants of lasting retrograde amnesia:
- **Bilateral brief pulse:** about 18–22% item loss on autobiographical interviews at 3–6 months, after allowing for normal forgetting (Jelovac) or as raw errors (Sackeim 2008).
- **High-dose brief-pulse RUL:** about 7–10%.
- **Ultrabrief RUL:** about 6% raw error at 6 months, comparable to ultrabrief bilateral and with no significant impairment versus baseline in Sackeim 2008.

Anterograde differences mostly disappear after 3 days. Ultrabrief RUL trades a modest loss in efficacy (about 1 fewer remission per 12 patients, and about 1 more session) for fewer cognitive effects.

### Cited Findings
- **Sackeim 2008 RCT (n=90; RUL 6× ST, bilateral 2.5× ST; 0.3 ms versus 1.5 ms):**
  - Remission: ultrabrief RUL 73%, brief-pulse bilateral 65%, brief-pulse RUL 59%, ultrabrief bilateral 35%.
  - Acutely, brief pulse was worse than ultrabrief on mMMS, Buschke delayed recall, complex-figure recall and retrograde memory (all p≤0.007).
  - Ultrabrief RUL "did not show a statistically significant impairment on any of the measures… relative to their own pre-ECT baseline."
  - 6-month AMI error rates: >22% (brief-pulse bilateral), 10% (brief-pulse RUL), 6% (ultrabrief bilateral), 6% (ultrabrief RUL) — [PMC2742986](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742986/).
- **Tor et al. 2015, *J Clin Psychiatry* (6 studies, n=689).** Brief versus ultrabrief RUL:
  - Brief pulse more efficacious: SMD 0.25 (0.08–0.41); ultrabrief remission OR 0.71 (0.51–0.99); NNT 12.1.
  - Sessions: 8.7 (brief) versus 9.6 (ultrabrief).
  - Brief pulse produced "significantly more cognitive side effects in all cognitive domains examined (global cognition, anterograde learning and recall, retrograde memory) (P < .01)". Cognitive SMDs were not given in the abstract — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26213985/).
- **Dutch multicentre RCT (PubMed 24330903; RUL 8× ST twice weekly, n=116).** Remission 68.4% (brief) versus 49.0% (ultrabrief); 7.1 versus 9.2 sessions; **no significant cognitive differences** (AMI, fluency) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24330903/). This conflicts with Tor 2015 on cognition.
- **Kolshus 2017 meta-analysis (7 RCTs, n=792): bitemporal (1–2.5× ST) versus high-dose RUL (>5× ST), brief pulse.**
  - No efficacy difference (g −0.03); 12-month relapse RR 1.42 (0.90–2.23).
  - RUL better on reorientation time (−8.28 min) and **AM (g −0.46, −0.87 to −0.04; I²=83%)**.
  - No difference in global cognition (g −0.03) or delayed verbal memory (g −0.10).
  - The only trial using 8× ST RUL showed no AM advantage, suggesting no benefit in exceeding about 6× ST — [PDF](https://www.stpatricks.ie/media/1839/bitemporal-v-high-dose-right-unilateral-electroconvulsive-therapy-for-depression-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-randomized-controlled-trials.pdf).
- **EFFECT-Dep:** AMI-SF bitemporal versus RUL OR 0.66 (end), 0.59 (3 months), 0.59 (6 months); reorientation 26.4 versus 19.1 min (median) — [AJP](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15030372).
- **Jelovac 2025:** ECT-attributable AMI-SF loss at 3 months: RUL 6× brief pulse −9.4%; bitemporal 1.5× brief pulse −21.0% — [PMC12052575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12052575/).
- **Mathiassen 2025:** AM SMD RUL 0.32 versus bilateral 0.82; NNH 5 — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/autobiographical-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/8F694868E2767C11F75DBA2C341C21AF).
- **Semkovska 2011:** RUL advantages in global cognition, delayed verbal recall and AM up to 3 days; no placement differences after 3 days on anterograde measures — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21109254/).
- **Sackeim 2007:** bilateral treatment count still predicted AM loss at 6 months; RUL and bifrontal did not. Sine wave caused reaction-time slowing at 6 months — [full text PDF](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf).
- **Subjective:** brief versus ultrabrief pulse OR 1.61 for subjective worsening at 1 week ([Brus 2017](http://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/yct50139-96.103.pdf)). Technical factors were not associated with subjective worsening at 6 months ([Tornhamre 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S016503272402007X)).

### Inferences
- **Well established:** bilateral brief-pulse ECT carries clearly the highest risk of lasting retrograde amnesia. High-dose RUL roughly halves it, and ultrabrief RUL reduces it further, to levels hard to distinguish from baseline in the best RCT.
- **Uncertain:** whether ultrabrief RUL's cognitive advantage holds at 6+ months against depressed controls; the Dutch RCT found no cognitive difference between brief and ultrabrief RUL. Also uncertain is the efficacy trade-off for a highly treatment-resistant patient, who may need more sessions or a switch to bilateral. Treatment number itself predicts AM loss.

### Gaps
- ELEKT-D's electrode placement and pulse-width mix was not retrieved, so its HVLT-R decline cannot be tied to technique.
- Tor 2015 cognitive effect sizes by time point were not retrieved.
- No head-to-head ultrabrief RUL versus brief-pulse RUL data with ≥6-month AM and depressed controls.
