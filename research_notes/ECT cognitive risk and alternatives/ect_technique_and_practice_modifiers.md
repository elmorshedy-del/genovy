# ECT Technique, Procedure and Practitioner Choices: How They Modify Cognitive Side Effects and Antidepressant Efficacy

Method note: The research used Exa and Tavily for search and extraction. The Scite MCP tool was requested but returned "MCP tools require a paid scite plan or an active free trial", so no Scite verification was possible. Wherever possible, numbers below were checked against primary abstracts or full texts (PMC, PubMed, journal pages). Secondary sources are labelled. Patient context: a young adult with about 10 years of treatment-resistant depression (TRD) who has failed all antidepressant classes and rTMS, is very prone to side effects (psychomotor slowing, brain fog), and plans to attend medical school. Magnetic seizure therapy, FEAT and other novel seizure therapies are out of scope; another researcher covers them.

Abbreviations: BT = bitemporal; BF = bifrontal; BL = bilateral (usually BT); RUL = right unilateral (d'Elia); LART = left anterior right temporal; UB = ultrabrief pulse (0.25–0.3 ms); BP = brief pulse (0.5–1.5 ms); ST = seizure threshold; AMI-SF/CUAMI-SF = Columbia Autobiographical Memory Interview–Short Form; HRSD/HAM-D = Hamilton Depression Rating Scale; MADRS = Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale.

## 1. Electrode placement (BT vs BF vs RUL vs LART): remission and cognition

### Takeaway
When RUL is dosed high (5–8× ST), its remission rate is statistically indistinguishable from moderate-dose BT; a 7-RCT meta-analysis put them at about 52% vs 53%. RUL clearly costs less cognitively: reorientation is about 8 minutes faster, and autobiographical (retrograde) memory loss is smaller, with the difference persisting to 6 months. BT works faster in the first 2–6 sessions. BF is about as effective as BT, but its cognitive advantage is inconsistent. LART has no RCT evidence.

### Cited Findings
**Kellner et al. 2010 (CORE, Br J Psychiatry 196:226–34)**
- Design: multicentre, double-blind RCT (NCT00069407; n=230), thrice weekly, Thymatron DGx. Arms were BF at 1.5× ST, BT at 1.5× ST and RUL at 6× ST. Remission rates were RUL 55% (95% CI 43–66%), BF 61% (50–71%) and BT 64% (53–75%). The differences were not statistically significant, and the authors note the study had low power to detect them — [Kellner 2010, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2830057/)
- Intention-to-treat table:
  - RUL: 54.6% remitted (42/77), 31.2% dropped out.
  - BF: 60.5% (49/81), 27.2% dropped out.
  - BT: 63.9% (46/72), 23.6% dropped out.
  - Source: [Kellner 2010, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/bifrontal-bitemporal-and-right-unilateral-electrode-placement-in-ect-randomised-trial/223FB4CD4A4C1D64312528A390AAC3B5)
- Speed and session counts:
  - BT produced faster symptom reduction: HRSD was significantly better than BF after sessions 2–3 and better than RUL after sessions 2–6.
  - End-of-treatment HRSD did not differ (13.1 RUL vs 11.5 BF vs 11.4 BT; p=0.418).
  - Mean ECT visits among remitters were 5.9 (RUL), 6.2 (BF) and 5.5 (BT).
  - Source: [BMJ Evidence-Based Mental Health summary](https://mentalhealth.bmj.com/content/13/3/85)
- Cognition: there were few cognitive differences between placements. BF was significantly worse than BT on two anterograde memory measures (AVLT 1–5, AVLT delay). The authors conclude that their data "do not support a cognitive advantage of bifrontal over bitemporal" — [Kellner 2010, Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/bifrontal-bitemporal-and-right-unilateral-electrode-placement-in-ect-randomised-trial/223FB4CD4A4C1D64312528A390AAC3B5)
- Device ceiling: US devices are limited to under 600 mC of charge output, which kept 5 of 77 RUL patients (6.5%) from receiving the full 6× ST dose — [Kellner 2010, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2830057/)

**Kolshus, Jelovac & McLoughlin 2017 meta-analysis (Psychol Med 47:518–530)**
- Scope: 7 RCTs (n=792) comparing high-dose (5–8× ST) brief-pulse RUL with low-to-moderate (1.0–2.5× ST) BT.
- Efficacy: no difference in depression change (Hedges g = −0.03, 95% CI −0.17 to 0.11), remission (RR 1.06, 0.93–1.20) or 12-month relapse (RR 1.42, 0.90–2.23).
- Cognition favoured RUL:
  - Reorientation after each session was 8.28 minutes faster (95% CI −12.86 to −3.70).
  - Retrograde autobiographical memory was better (g = −0.46, −0.87 to −0.04).
  - There was no difference in general cognition, category fluency, or delayed visual or verbal memory.
- Source: [Kolshus 2017 full text](https://www.stpatricks.ie/media/1839/bitemporal-v-high-dose-right-unilateral-electroconvulsive-therapy-for-depression-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-randomized-controlled-trials.pdf)
- Relapse and heterogeneity details:
  - Pooled 1-year relapse was 34.6% (95% CI 22.6–48.7) after high-dose RUL vs 49.1% (35.6–62.8) after BT; the difference was not significant.
  - Heterogeneity in the autobiographical memory result was driven partly by McCall 2002, the only trial to use 8× ST RUL and the only one with a trend against RUL.
  - Source: [Kolshus 2017](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291716002737)
- A 2017 corrigendum fixed a figure in which the reorientation data for the two groups had been swapped; the conclusions were unchanged — [Corrigendum](https://exa.ai/library/publication/5vnkxcf9fyd)
- McLoughlin, letter to AJP (2016):
  - Pooled remission in these trials was about 52% (high-dose RUL) vs about 53% (BT).
  - Crossover data came from one trial (Sackeim 2009). Patients without substantial improvement after ≥8 sessions were switched to 2.5× ST BT. Of those originally on RUL, 15/31 (48%) remitted; of those originally on BT, 11/29 (38%) remitted.
  - He recommends starting non-life-threatening TRD with high-dose RUL, then switching to BT at 1.5× ST if benefit is insufficient. He notes there is "no evidence that [2.5×] is better than 1.5× threshold bitemporal".
  - Source: [McLoughlin 2016, AJP letter](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16020252r)

**EFFECT-Dep (Semkovska et al. 2016, Am J Psychiatry 173:408–417)**
- Design: pragmatic, patient- and rater-blinded non-inferiority RCT (n=138), **twice weekly**, comparing RUL 6× ST with BT 1.5× ST. RUL was non-inferior on HAM-D-24 (mean difference 1.08 points favouring RUL; 95% CI −1.67 to 3.84) — [EFFECT-Dep, AJP](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15030372)
- Outcomes, RUL vs BT:
  - Response: 42/69 (60.8%) vs 35/69 (50.7%).
  - Remission: 32/69 (46.4%) vs 29/69 (42.0%).
  - 6-month relapse among remitters: 8/32 (25.0%) vs 11/29 (37.9%); not significant.
  - Median sessions were 7 for remitters and 9 for non-remitters.
  - Source: [EFFECT-Dep, AJP](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15030372)
- Cognition, RUL vs BT:
  - Median reorientation was 19.1 vs 26.4 minutes.
  - BT had lower autobiographical-memory consistency (OR 0.66, 95% CI 0.513–0.85), and the gap persisted at 3 months (OR 0.59) and 6 months (OR 0.59).
  - RUL also showed better verbal learning and fewer subjective cognitive complaints.
  - Source: [EFFECT-Dep, AJP](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15030372)

**Sackeim et al. 1993 (NEJM 328:839–46), n=96**
- Response by arm: low-dose RUL (just above ST) 17%, high-dose RUL (2.5× ST) 43%, low-dose BL 65%, high-dose BL 63%.
- Reorientation: high-dose RUL took 83% longer to reorient than low-dose RUL; BL took 252% longer.
- Memory: BL produced three times more retrograde amnesia for personal information in the week after treatment. There were no group differences at 2 months.
- Relapse: 41/70 responders (59%) relapsed, with no group difference.
- Source: [Sackeim 1993, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8441428/)

**Sackeim et al. 2000 (Arch Gen Psychiatry 57:425–434), 4 groups of about 20**
- RUL at 1.5× ST and 2.5× ST gave remission of 35% and 30%. Higher-dose RUL gave about 60%, vs 65% for BL at 2.5× ST — [Psychiatric Times (secondary)](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/new-research-improves-ect-outcomes)
- Kellner 2010 independently cites these figures as RUL vs BT remission of 60% vs 65% — [Kellner 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2830057/)

**Sackeim et al. 2009 (OPT-ECT, Arch Gen Psychiatry 66:729–737), n=319**
- High-dose RUL "did not differ or was superior" to BL in efficacy. The remission advantage for RUL ranged from 8.5% to 13.2% depending on the completer definition, and RUL caused less amnesia (AMI-SF, Buschke SRT) — [Sackeim 2009, JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/483117)

**Bifrontal (Dunne & McLoughlin 2012 meta-analysis, World J Biol Psychiatry 13:248–58)**
- Scope: 8 RCTs (n=617).
- Efficacy: BF equalled BT (g = 0.102, CI −0.110 to 0.313) and equalled RUL (SMD −0.12).
- Cognition was mixed:
  - MMSE declined less with BF than BT (g = 0.89, CI 0.054–1.724).
  - RUL impaired complex-figure recall more than BF (g = 0.76).
  - BF impaired word recall more than RUL (g = −1.45, CI −2.75 to −0.15).
- Source: [Dunne 2012, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22098115/)

**Ultrabrief BF vs ultrabrief RUL (Sienaert et al. 2009/2010; n=81; BF 1.5× ST vs RUL 6× ST)**
- Response was 78% in both arms. Remission (HRSD ≤10) was 59% BF vs 72% RUL; using HRSD ≤7, it was 34% vs 44%. None of the differences were significant — [Psychiatric Times (secondary)](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/ultrabrief-pulse-right-unilateral-ect-new-standard-care)

**Semkovska et al. 2011 meta-analysis (J Psychiatr Res 45:770–80)**
- Scope: 39 studies, 1,415 patients.
- Up to 3 days after the last treatment, unilateral ECT caused smaller decreases than BT in global cognition, delayed verbal recall and autobiographical memory.
- Higher unilateral dose predicted larger acute decreases in verbal learning and delayed recall.
- Beyond 3 days there were no placement differences, and dose no longer predicted cognition.
- Source: [Semkovska 2011, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21109254/)

**LART**
- Evidence so far:
  - Proposed by Swartz; a 2005 review argues on clinical and biomedical principles — [Swartz & Nelson 2005](https://exa.ai/library/publication/cp6fnn3cd7z)
  - A 2020 computational modelling study found that, compared with BT, LART gave greater left cingulate stimulation and relatively less bilateral hippocampal stimulation. It calls LART "promising for further clinical investigation" — [LART modelling study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925492720301293)
  - It is used by 20% of sites in a 2022 international survey — [Rohde et al., ECT Across Nations](https://exa.ai/library/publication/yl7gv657xgh)

**Guideline positions**
- RANZCP (2019):
  - BT "carries the highest risk of retrograde amnesia and is not used as the initial form of ECT treatment given, unless there are specific reasons", such as urgency or prior non-response.
  - BF "should be considered" in patients at risk of ECT-induced arrhythmias.
  - Source: [RANZCP 2019 guideline, full text](https://www.psychiatry.org/File%20Library/Psychiatrists/Practice/Clinical%20Practice%20Guidelines/ANZJP-Practice-Guidelines-for-ECT.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a non-urgent, cognitively vulnerable TRD patient, the evidence supports starting with high-dose RUL. BT's only consistent advantage is speed in sessions 2–6, and it consistently costs more autobiographical memory, with an effect still detectable at 6 months in EFFECT-Dep.
- If a switch to bilateral ECT is needed, BT at 1.5× ST (not 2.5×) is the evidence-based next step. BF is a reasonable alternative but is not clearly kinder to cognition.
- LART should be treated as experimental; there are no outcome RCTs.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head RCT of UB-RUL vs BT in young adults with chronic TRD was found.
- No RCT of LART was found.
- The retrograde-amnesia numbers for Sackeim 2000 were not retrieved, and its efficacy numbers come from a secondary source (Psychiatric Times), corroborated by Kellner 2010.

## 2. Pulse width: ultrabrief (0.25–0.3 ms) vs brief (0.5–1.5 ms)

### Takeaway
UB-RUL reliably reduces acute and short-term cognitive effects, especially reorientation time and autobiographical memory. It costs a small amount of efficacy:
- In the Tor 2015 meta-analysis, SMD was 0.25 in favour of brief pulse, the remission OR was 0.71 (one extra remission per about 12 patients treated with brief pulse), and UB needed about 1 extra session (9.6 vs 8.7).
- UB can match brief pulse if the UB dose is raised to 8× ST vs 5× for brief (Loo 2015).
- UB is not recommended for bilateral placements; UB-BL had only 35% remission.

### Cited Findings
**Tor et al. 2015 meta-analysis (J Clin Psychiatry 76:1092–98)**
- Scope: 6 studies, 689 patients.
- Efficacy: brief-pulse RUL was more efficacious (SMD 0.25, 95% CI 0.08–0.41; p=.004). UB had lower remission (OR 0.71, 0.51–0.99; NNT 12.1). Mean sessions were 8.7 (brief) vs 9.6 (UB) (p<.001).
- Cognition: brief pulse caused more cognitive side effects in all domains examined — global cognition, anterograde learning and recall, and retrograde memory (p<.01).
- Source: [Tor 2015, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26213985/)
- Senior author Loo's comment: "Patients with a very resistant form of depression that is difficult to treat may still require standard (brief pulse) ECT, which is slightly more effective" — [Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/848433)

**Sackeim et al. 2008 (Brain Stimulation 1:71–83), double-masked, n=90; RUL 6× ST or BL 2.5× ST at 0.3 ms or 1.5 ms**
- Remission 1 week after ECT: UB-RUL 73%, BP-RUL 59%, BP-BL 65%, UB-BL 35%.
- Mean treatments: UB-RUL 8.7, UB-BL 8.9, BP-RUL 8.5, BP-BL 6.2.
- Time to reorientation, compared with UB-RUL: UB-BL took 36% longer, BP-RUL 108% longer and BP-BL 212% longer.
- Within-patient change: "The ultrabrief RUL ECT group did not show a statistically significant impairment on any of the measures obtained during or immediately following the ECT course relative to their own pre-ECT baseline."
- Autobiographical amnesia: the UB advantage persisted to 6 months. More treatments and older age were associated with greater retrograde amnesia.
- Patient report: 71% of all patients reported a negative effect on memory. UB-RUL patients gave the least negative ratings.
- Source: [Sackeim 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742986/)

**Spaans et al. 2013 (J Clin Psychiatry 74:e1029–36), n=116, twice weekly, RUL at 8× ST, 1.0 ms vs 0.3–0.4 ms, up to 6 weeks**
- Among completers, remission was 68.4% (26/58) with brief pulse vs 49.0% (24/49) with UB (p=.019).
- Sessions to remission were 7.1 vs 9.2 (p=.008).
- No significant cognitive differences were found.
- Source: [Spaans 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24330903/)
- **Critique (Knapp et al., letter)**:
  - The ITT decrease in MADRS was identical between groups.
  - 6 brief-pulse patients (10%) dropped out for confusion or delirium vs 0 in the UB arm; this may have biased the cognitive comparison.
  - Completion was 65.5% (brief) vs 84.5% (UB).
  - Twice-weekly dosing at 8× ST may have reduced UB efficacy.
  - Source: [Knapp letter, J Clin Psychiatry 2014](https://www.psychiatrist.com/jcp/brief-pulse-ultrabrief-pulse-right-unilateral-electroconvulsive/)

**Loo et al. 2015 (Int J Neuropsychopharmacol 18:pyu045), double-blind RCT, n=102; UB-RUL 8× ST vs BP-RUL 5× ST**
- Efficacy was not significantly different, including speed (number of treatments).
- During the course, UB caused less impairment in orientation and autobiographical memory (AMI-SF).
- No significant group differences remained at 1 and 6 months.
- Source: [Loo 2015, IJNP](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/18/1/pyu045/651640)
- The same paper notes that naturalistic data (Loo 2008, 2012; Galletly 2013) and a speed-of-response analysis (Loo 2013) suggest UB-RUL needs more treatments even at slightly higher doses. It also notes that highly ECT-responsive patients (older, psychotic) may respond equally to either pulse width, while differences may emerge in less ECT-responsive patients — [Loo 2015, IJNP](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/18/1/pyu045/651640)

**Swedish national registry (Q-ECT)**
- **Within one week** (n=1,212; J ECT 2017; DOI 10.1097/YCT.0000000000000377):
  - Subjective memory worsening (≥2 points on CPRS memory item) occurred in 26% overall.
  - Women 31% vs men 18%; age 18–39 32% vs ≥65 22%.
  - Brief pulse vs UB in unilateral ECT: adjusted OR 1.61 (95% CI 1.05–2.47).
  - Remission was associated with less memory worsening.
  - Source: [J ECT 2017](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/ectjournal/fulltext/10.1097/yct.0000000000000377~subjective-memory-immediately-following-electroconvulsive)
- **At 6 months** (n=312, unilateral):
  - No difference in subjective memory worsening or remission across pulse widths <0.5, 0.5 and >0.5 ms.
  - Remission at 6 months was 18.6% in ages 16–39 vs 62.2% in ≥65 (OR for non-remission 5.02, 2.46–10.28).
  - Source: [J ECT 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7676462/)
- **HRQoL study** (n=5,046):
  - No robust association between pulse width and health-related quality of life.
  - Swedish practice uses age-based dosing (about 5 mC per year of age) with 0.5 ms as the median pulse width.
  - Source: [Ernstsson et al. 2023](https://exa.ai/library/publication/mxb5p7zxc3k)
- **Ongoing RCT**: a national register-based RCT of 0.5 ms vs 1.0 ms (n=800 planned; NCT04057378) had a primary completion date of Nov 2025. No published results were found as of Oct 2026 — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04057378)

**RANZCP 2019**
- Recommended pairings:
  - RUL: UB 0.3 ms at 6× ST; 1.0 ms at 5–6× ST; or 0.5 ms at 5–6× ST (lower level of evidence).
  - BF/BT: 1.0 ms at 1.5× ST, or 0.5 ms at 1.5–2.5× ST.
- On ultrabrief bilateral ECT: evidence "is very limited… efficacy is lower than with brief-pulse bilateral ECT".
- Modelling suggests 0.5 ms effects are intermediate between 1.0 ms and 0.3 ms.
- Source: [RANZCP 2019](https://www.psychiatry.org/File%20Library/Psychiatrists/Practice/Clinical%20Practice%20Guidelines/ANZJP-Practice-Guidelines-for-ECT.pdf)

**PRIDE Phase 1 (Kellner 2016, Am J Psychiatry; age ≥60; UB-RUL 6× ST thrice weekly plus venlafaxine; n=240)**
- Remission was 61.7%, response 70%, and dropout 28.3%; mean treatments to remission were 7.3 (SD 3.1) — [PRIDE Phase 1](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15081101)
- Pooled CORE data for comparison in people ≥60: BL ECT gave 69.7% remission and BP-RUL gave 70.4% (n=27) — [PRIDE Phase 1](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15081101)

### Inferences
- **Remission gap.** The UB vs BP gap is real but modest: roughly 0 to 20 percentage points across trials, depending on design. It is near zero when UB is given at 8× ST (Loo 2015) or thrice weekly (Sackeim 2008), and about 19 points when UB is given twice weekly at 8× ST (Spaans 2013, completers).
- **Extra sessions.** UB needs about 1–2 extra sessions on average (meta-analysis 8.7 vs 9.6; Spaans 7.1 vs 9.2).
- **Persistent harm.** Whether UB prevents *persistent* subjective memory problems is less certain. RCTs show an objective autobiographical-memory advantage to 6 months (Sackeim 2008); Loo 2015 and Swedish registry data at 6 months show no difference.
- **Implication for this patient.** For a very side-effect-prone patient, UB-RUL at ≥6× ST (consider 8×) is the most cognition-sparing evidence-based start. Because highly treatment-resistant patients may be the group where brief pulse's slight efficacy edge matters, there should be a pre-agreed rule to move to 0.5–1.0 ms RUL if there is no response after about 6–8 UB sessions.

### Gaps
- Mayur 2013 (J Affect Disord) numbers were not retrieved; Loo 2015 reports that it found no significant efficacy difference.
- There are no RCT data for 0.5 ms vs 0.3 ms RUL; the Swedish 0.5 vs 1.0 ms RCT has not yet published.
- No data specific to young adults with decade-long TRD were found.

## 3. Dose relative to seizure threshold; titration vs age-based or formula dosing

### Takeaway
RUL efficacy depends steeply on dose: response was 17% at about 1× ST, 43% at 2.5×, and about 60–73% at 6× ST, and higher dose also costs more acute cognition. BT/BL works at 1.5× ST, and going higher mainly speeds response while adding cognitive cost. ST varies widely between individuals (6- to 40-fold reported). Age-based or fixed dosing leaves a large share of patients badly over- or under-dosed, so empirical titration is the standard at quality centres.

### Cited Findings
- **RUL dose–response:**
  - 1× ST 17%, 2.5× ST 43% (Sackeim 1993) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8441428/)
  - 1.5× 35%, 2.5× 30%, high dose about 60% (Sackeim 2000) — [Psychiatric Times](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/new-research-improves-ect-outcomes)
  - 6× about 55–73% (Kellner 2010; Sackeim 2008) — [Kellner 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2830057/); [Sackeim 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742986/)
- **Speed and orientation:** "Regardless of electrode placement, high dosage resulted in more rapid improvement." High-dose RUL took 83% longer to reorient than low-dose RUL — [Sackeim 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8441428/)
- **Acute cost of higher RUL dose:** larger acute decreases in verbal learning, delayed verbal recall, visual recognition and semantic memory retrieval, but no dose effect beyond 3 days after ECT — [Semkovska 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21109254/)
- **Bilateral dose:** "Doses up to six times threshold can maximise the efficacy of unilateral ECT, while for bilateral ECT, doses at 1.5 times seizure threshold are usually sufficient"; higher doses carry greater cognitive side effects — [RANZCP 2019](https://www.psychiatry.org/File%20Library/Psychiatrists/Practice/Clinical%20Practice%20Guidelines/ANZJP-Practice-Guidelines-for-ECT.pdf)
  - McLoughlin: 2.5× BT "increases cognitive side effects" and there is no evidence it beats 1.5× — [McLoughlin 2016](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16020252r)
- **Lower RUL multiples:** "Lower dose levels (3× threshold) have been shown to have lower, though still clinically meaningful efficacy" — [RANZCP 2019 Table 1](https://www.psychiatry.org/File%20Library/Psychiatrists/Practice/Clinical%20Practice%20Guidelines/ANZJP-Practice-Guidelines-for-ECT.pdf)
- **Titration vs age-based estimates (Tiller & Ingram 2006, n=203):**
  - Age-based dosing would have given excessive initial stimuli to 30% of females and 8% of males.
  - Ineffective doses would have gone to 2% of females and 7% of males with full-age dosing, and to 64% with half-age dosing.
  - Conclusion: for high-dose RUL, initial ST should be titrated.
  - Source: [ANZJP 2006](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/j.1440-1614.2006.01773.x)
- **Ittasakul et al. 2019:**
  - For RUL, only 31% (half-age) and 22% (age method) of patients would have received a dose within ±20% of the titrated dose.
  - The age method produced up to 17.2× ST for RUL.
  - Initial ST varied 6.7-fold for RUL and 18-fold for BL.
  - Source: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369840/)
- **Laidlaw 2000:**
  - ST ranged from 25 to 403 mC.
  - Estimates were acceptable in 33% of cases with the age method, 64% with half-age and 40% with fixed dose.
  - A fixed dose of 275 mC would have been excessive in 57.5%.
  - The review cites 40-fold ST variation in research populations and 6–12-fold in clinical groups.
  - Source: [Psychiatric Bulletin](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychiatric-bulletin/article/comparison-of-stimulus-dosing-methods-for-electroconvulsive-therapy/D5B32397CD83EDA3C7AEA8A5F5911BC4)
- **Contrary data (CORE, n=402, bilateral):**
  - Little titrated-ST variability was observed, possibly because the titration steps were widely spaced.
  - Half-age estimates averaged 18% above ST.
  - Source: [Petrides 2009, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19972637/)
- **Factors affecting ST:** electrode placement, sex, age and anaesthetic agent; "caution should be exercised when using nonindividualized methods" (n=503) — [Lemasson 2018](https://exa.ai/library/publication/c71k8g5d00t)
- **Current practice:**
  - In a 2022 international survey, ST titration was preferred for RUL by 82% of sites, and the median therapeutic multiple was 6× ST for RUL and 2× for BT/BF — [Rohde et al.](https://exa.ai/library/publication/yl7gv657xgh)
  - In a 2022 US survey, no site used fixed dosing — [US 2022 survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11136609/)
  - Sweden routinely uses age-based dosing (about 5 mC per year) — [Ernstsson 2023](https://exa.ai/library/publication/mxb5p7zxc3k)

### Inferences
- With formula dosing, a young woman's RUL treatment can easily land at 10–17× ST, causing unnecessary cognitive load. Titration at session 1, followed by an explicitly stated multiple (e.g., "6× ST RUL"), should be a non-negotiable request.
- Because young people tend to have low thresholds, titrated 6× ST will usually be well within device limits.
- Under-dosing (<2.5× for RUL) wastes sessions, and every extra session adds cognitive load, so under-dosing is not "cognition-sparing".

### Gaps
- McCall et al. 2000 (titrated 2.25× vs fixed high-dose RUL) exact numbers were not retrieved.
- No RCT directly compares titration with age-based dosing on clinical or cognitive outcomes; the evidence is dose-accuracy modelling.

## 4. Treatment frequency (3×, 2×, 1× weekly)

### Takeaway
Final efficacy is equivalent for twice- and thrice-weekly treatment. Thrice-weekly works faster in calendar time; twice-weekly adds about 5–6 days to the course and may need slightly fewer sessions. Thrice-weekly causes more cognitive impairment. Once-weekly is clearly less effective.

### Cited Findings
- **Lerer et al. 1995 (Am J Psychiatry 152:564–70)**: double-blind, n=52, bilateral brief-pulse. Final HAM-D outcomes were equal at 1 week and 1 month, but response was "significantly faster" with thrice weekly, and "cognitive effects were more prominent with ECT three times a week" — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7694905/)
- **Shapira et al. 1998 (Br J Psychiatry 172:44–8)**: up to 8 bilateral ECTs. Outcomes were equivalent; response was faster with thrice weekly but "associated with more severe memory impairment". Twice weekly is "an optimum schedule… unless clinical indications require the more rapid antidepressant effect" — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9534831/)
- **Charlson et al. 2012 meta-analysis (J Affect Disord 138:1–8)**: 8 datasets, 214 subjects.
  - Change in depression was similar for twice vs thrice weekly (SMD −0.11 to −0.17).
  - Twice weekly lengthened treatment by 4.78–6.48 days.
  - There was a trend toward fewer real ECTs with twice weekly.
  - Thrice weekly was more effective than once weekly (SMD 1.25–1.31).
  - Source: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21501875/)
- **UK ECT Review Group (Lancet 2003)**: "a trend towards greater cognitive impairment from more frequent ECT administration (210 participants in 6 trials)" — [BMJ EBMH summary](https://mentalhealth.bmj.com/content/6/3/83)
- **McAllister et al. 1987**: unilateral ECT twice vs thrice weekly (n=20) gave equivalent improvement, but "visual memory impairment was significantly lower in the twice-weekly group" — [Psychiatry Res](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0165178187900631)
- **Spacing as a cognitive lever**:
  - Martin 2015 lists wider spacing among strategies to reduce retrograde amnesia — [Martin 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4675978/)
  - RANZCP lists "spacing ECT treatments further apart" as an action when reorientation monitoring signals emerging amnesia — [RANZCP 2019](https://www.psychiatry.org/File%20Library/Psychiatrists/Practice/Clinical%20Practice%20Guidelines/ANZJP-Practice-Guidelines-for-ECT.pdf)
- **Current US practice**: 85% of sites give 3 sessions per week for depressive episodes, and the median course is 10 sessions — [US 2022 survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11136609/)

### Inferences
- **Twice weekly is a low-cost cognitive lever** for a non-suicidal, non-urgent patient: the same final outcome, about 1 extra week of treatment, and less cognitive impairment.
- **Interaction with UB pulse:** the only RCT that combined twice-weekly treatment with UB-RUL (Spaans) had the lowest UB remission rate (49%). The best UB results (Sackeim 2008, 73%; PRIDE, 62%) used thrice weekly. Combining all cognition-sparing levers at once — UB plus twice weekly plus modest dose — may compound slower response.
- **Practical sequence:** a reasonable plan is UB-RUL thrice weekly with spacing as the rescue lever if cognition worsens, or BP-RUL twice weekly (as in EFFECT-Dep).

### Gaps
- No RCT has directly crossed frequency with pulse width.
- Frequency data come mostly from bilateral ECT in the 1980s–1990s.

## 5. Number of sessions, stopping rules and switching (RUL→BT)

### Takeaway
Cognitive cost is cumulative. Each additional bilateral treatment linearly increases autobiographical amnesia that remains detectable at 6 months, whereas RUL and BF sessions show no 6-month dose–response. Most remitters need about 6–9 sessions. Good practice:
- Stop at remission, or at a plateau with no further gain after 2 more treatments.
- Change technique after about 4–6 (brief pulse) or 6–8 (UB) sessions without response.
- Get a second opinion beyond 12 sessions.

Stopping too early is also harmful: community remission rates were only 30–47%, partly because courses were ended prematurely.

### Cited Findings
- **Cumulative amnesia (Sackeim 2007, community study, n=347):**
  - AMI-SF scores declined linearly with treatment number for all placements, but the slope was steeper for BL (0.14) than RUL (0.06) or BF (0.09).
  - "The magnitude of long-term retrograde amnesia linearly increased with longer courses of BL ECT, but was unrelated to the number of RUL or BF treatments administered" at 6 months.
  - 38/306 (12.4%) patients met a priori criteria for marked and persistent retrograde amnesia.
  - Sources: [Sackeim 2007, Neuropsychopharmacology](https://www.nature.com/articles/1301180); full-text details from a [hosted copy of the paper](https://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/23.-Sackeim-H.-et-al.2007.-The-cognitive-effects-of-ECT-in-community-settings.pdf)
- **Sackeim 2008**: at 2 and 6 months, "more treatments and advancing age were associated with greater retrograde amnesia" — [Sackeim 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742986/)
- **Typical course lengths:**
  - EFFECT-Dep: median 7 sessions for remitters vs 9 for non-remitters — [EFFECT-Dep](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15030372)
  - CORE: mean 5.5–6.2 visits among remitters — [BMJ EBMH](https://mentalhealth.bmj.com/content/13/3/85)
  - PRIDE: 7.3 treatments to remission — [PRIDE](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15081101)
- **APA Task Force 2001 (secondary summary):**
  - "The treatment course should end as soon as maximal improvement is reached".
  - "Termination should be considered in patients who have shown substantial but incomplete clinical improvement yet remain unchanged after two additional treatment sessions".
  - Source: [Espinoza, ECT in the elderly (secondary)](https://teams.semel.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/course/Espinoza_ECT%20in%20the%20elderly.pdf)
- **RANZCP 2019 (via secondary summary):**
  - Switch from brief-pulse unilateral to BF or BT after 4–6 treatments without improvement.
  - Switch from UB-RUL to brief-pulse RUL, BF or BT after 6–8 treatments.
  - Re-titrate threshold when changing technique.
  - Courses are usually 6–12 sessions, and a second opinion is recommended if more than 12 are needed.
  - Source: [PsychScene RANZCP summary (secondary)](https://psychscenehub.com/psychinsights/electroconvulsive-therapy-summary-of-ranzcp-guidelines)
- **Queensland Health guideline**:
  - If response is inadequate after 6 adequately dosed treatments (up to 8 if UB), change to bilateral, or to BF if there is concern about cognitive impairment with BT.
  - If RUL causes unusual early cognitive impairment, consider BF or left unilateral.
  - Source: [Queensland Health ECT guideline](https://www.health.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0028/444763/guideline-administration-electroconvulsive-therapy.pdf)
- **BJPsych Advances review**: the mean number of treatments in most research and clinical practice is 8–10, and slightly more with UB-RUL. "Wherever clinically possible, prolonged courses (>12 treatments) should be avoided". Clinically, cognitive effects "sometimes rapidly become more problematic" late in a course, which the authors describe as a threshold effect — [PMC7191622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622)
- **US practice (2022 survey)**:
  - Median of 6 treatments with little or no response before adjusting technique.
  - Almost all sites (97%) require about 12 treatments before calling a patient a non-responder.
  - The most common adjustments are RUL→BT (65%), RUL→BF (30%) and UB→brief pulse (30%).
  - Source: [US 2022 survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11136609/)
- **Crossover outcomes**: patients switched from 6× RUL to 2.5× BT after ≥8 sessions had 48% remission (15/31) — [McLoughlin 2016](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16020252r)
- **Premature stopping (Prudic 2004, 7 community hospitals, n=347):**
  - Remission was 30.3–46.7% depending on criteria, vs the 70–90% expected.
  - "Providers frequently end the ECT course with the view that patients have benefited fully, yet formal assessment shows significant residual symptoms".
  - Only 23.4% of non-remitters had sustained remission during follow-up.
  - Source: [Prudic 2004, Biol Psychiatry](https://www.biologicalpsychiatryjournal.com/article/S0006-3223(03)01046-1/abstract)
- **Remission and subjective memory**: in the Swedish registry, remission was associated with less subjective memory worsening (OR 0.29 at 6 months). The authors note that "an ineffective or too short a treatment series might therefore increase the risk" of subjective memory worsening — [PMC7676462](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7676462/); [J ECT 2017](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/ectjournal/fulltext/10.1097/yct.0000000000000377~subjective-memory-immediately-following-electroconvulsive)

### Inferences
- **Cognitive load is per-session and placement-specific.** With RUL or BF, extra sessions add acute load but showed no 6-month dose–response in the large community study; with BL they did. This argues for exhausting RUL options (UB, then BP) before switching to BT, and for keeping any BT phase short.
- **Pre-specify the rules.** A patient should agree in advance on objective remission criteria (e.g., MADRS ≤10 on two consecutive ratings), a plateau rule (no further gain after 2 more sessions), switch points (6–8 UB sessions, 4–6 BP sessions) and a ceiling (a review at 12 sessions).

### Gaps
- No RCT has tested "stop at remission" against a fixed number of sessions.
- No study was found that quantifies cognitive load per session for UB-RUL beyond the end of the course.

## 6. Anaesthesia, seizure-quality adjuncts, concurrent medications and cognitive-protective drugs

### Takeaway
- **Anaesthetic choice** (methohexital, propofol, etomidate, thiopental, ketamine) has little proven effect on remission or cognition. Propofol shortens seizures, and methohexital or etomidate give longer seizures. Ketamine, as anaesthetic or add-on, failed in two RCTs (KANECT and Ketamine-ECT).
- **Seizure quality** improves with a longer interval between anaesthetic induction and the stimulus. Hyperventilation lengthens seizures by a few seconds, but outcome benefit is unproven.
- **Concurrent medication** matters:
  - Nortriptyline during ECT raised remission by about 15 percentage points and reduced cognitive effects; venlafaxine tended to worsen cognition.
  - Lithium during ECT was associated with about 12-fold higher odds of delirium in a national sample.
  - Anticonvulsants were associated with worse response.
  - Benzodiazepine data are conflicting.
- **Cognitive-protective drugs:** memantine and liothyronine (T3) have low-quality evidence of short-term benefit; none has been tested on persistent autobiographical memory.

### Cited Findings
**Anaesthetic agents**
- **Cochrane review 2014** (18 RCTs, n=599): no difference in depression reduction between propofol and methohexital. Propofol shortened EEG and motor seizures. "If it is difficult to elicit an adequately long seizure, methohexital may be superior to propofol (low-quality evidence)" — [Cochrane CD009763](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD009763_different-regimens-intravenous-sedatives-or-hypnotics-electroconvulsive-therapy-ect-adult-patients)
- **Fond et al. 2016 Bayesian network meta-analysis** (14 double-blind RCTs, n=610):
  - Methohexital gave better depression scores than propofol after excluding heterogeneous trials; this rested on only 2 RCTs (p=0.001).
  - Methohexital gave longer seizures than propofol.
  - There was no difference across the 6 agents in responder or dropout rates.
  - Ketamine "cannot be recommended to date".
  - Source: [Sci Rep 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep19847)
- **Huoponen et al. 2025 meta-analysis** (3 RCTs, n=131 meta-analysed): no difference in the number of ECT sessions needed for remission between propofol and methohexital (Hedges g = 0.00, 95% CI −0.35 to 0.35); low-quality evidence — [Huoponen 2025](https://exa.ai/library/publication/86txcz024zw)
- **Etomidate vs propofol meta-analysis 2023** (16 studies): etomidate produced longer motor seizures (SMD 1.09) and EEG seizures (SMD 0.98). Etomidate carries adrenal suppression, myoclonus and nausea risks — [Frontiers in Neurology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2023.1251882/full)
- **Kaiser Permanente cohort** (n=495; etomidate vs methohexital):
  - No difference in 12-month remission (40% overall) or rehospitalisation.
  - Methohexital patients changed anaesthetic mid-course for seizure inadequacy in 21% vs 2% of etomidate patients.
  - Discomfort adverse effects occurred in 2.1–2.4% with etomidate vs 0.4% with methohexital.
  - Source: [PMC10578333](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10578333/)
- **Seizure duration by agent (Singh meta-analysis, cited in a narrative review):** etomidate gave EEG seizures 17.65 s longer than propofol and 11.81 s longer than thiopental, with no significant difference from methohexital. The review says methohexital "is considered by many to be the gold standard" — [PMC8466199](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8466199/)
- **KANECT** (double-blind RCT; ketamine up to 2 mg/kg vs propofol as sole anaesthetic): "No significant differences were found on any outcome measure during, at the end of or 1 month following the ECT course". This included the number of ECTs, HRSD/MADRS scores and CANTAB spatial recognition memory — [KANECT, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5451642/)
- **Ketamine-ECT (Anderson et al. 2017, Lancet Psychiatry)**: multicentre RCT, n=79, ketamine 0.5 mg/kg added to anaesthesia vs saline.
  - Primary outcome (delayed verbal recall, HVLT-R-DR) showed no benefit: difference −0.43 (95% CI −1.73 to 0.87).
  - AMI-SF effect size was −0.07 mid-course and −0.01 at the end.
  - Remission was 39% vs 35%; response 49% vs 60%.
  - Mean ECTs to remission were 10.0 vs 7.0 (not significant).
  - Adverse events: 45% vs 27%.
  - The trial excluded a benefit of effect size 0.3 or greater on the primary outcome.
  - Source: [Anderson 2017, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5406618/)
- **Earlier ketamine meta-analyses (narrative review)**: Ainsworth (18 double-blind RCTs) found ketamine did not improve depressive symptoms early or late and lengthened seizures; no significant neurocognitive benefit was found. Ren found no better response or remission, with more adverse events — [PMC8466199](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8466199/)

**Seizure-quality augmentation**
- **Anaesthetic-to-stimulus interval (Taylor et al. 2020, Brain Stimulation; crossover RCT, n=54)**:
  - A longer interval (2.5 vs 1.5 min) gave higher-quality seizures on all indices (amplitude, regularity, post-ictal suppression) and longer seizures (p<0.01).
  - Hyperventilation (about 25 breaths/min) did not affect seizure quality.
  - Neither factor changed recovery of orientation.
  - Source: [Taylor 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/r2hlfdjwb0p)
- **Hyperventilation meta-analysis 2025** (7 studies, 620 sessions):
  - Lengthened motor seizures by 2.88 s (95% CI 0.92–4.84) and EEG seizures by 5.43 s (0.66–10.20); GRADE moderate.
  - No safety differences.
  - "Lack of evidence regarding whether hyperventilation actually improves clinical outcomes".
  - Source: [BMC Psychiatry 2025](https://bmcpsychiatry.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12888-025-07105-7)
- **Remifentanil (Gálvez et al. 2016; 96 sessions, 36 patients, retrospective)**: adding remifentanil at an unchanged anaesthetic dose had no effect on any seizure quality parameter. Its value is in allowing a lower dose of the main anaesthetic — [Eur Arch Psychiatry Clin Neurosci](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00406-016-0690-9)

**Concurrent psychotropics**
- **Nortriptyline and venlafaxine (Sackeim 2009, OPT-ECT, triple-masked, n=319)**:
  - Nortriptyline during ECT raised remission by about 15 percentage points vs placebo (12.5–17.5% across completer definitions).
  - Nortriptyline was cognitively better than venlafaxine (MMSE, Buschke SRT) and than placebo (N-back).
  - Venlafaxine improved remission by 9.2–14.6% but "tended to worsen cognitive adverse effects".
  - Source: [JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/483117); [Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/705658)
- **Lithium (Patel et al. 2020, Brain Stimulation; US national inpatient sample)**:
  - Delirium occurred in 5.7% with ECT plus lithium (n=422) vs 0.6% with ECT alone (n=64,148): adjusted OR 11.7 (95% CI 7.55–17.99).
  - In unipolar depression, the delirium rate was 7.8%.
  - Cognitive impairment odds were 542% higher.
  - Source: [Brain Stimulation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1935861X19303584)
  - Elias, Thomas & Sackeim (2020 AJP review): adverse reactions tend to occur at lithium levels near or above 1.0 mEq/L; "a common practice is to hold the medication the day before" — [AJP 2020](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2020.20030238)
- **Anticonvulsants (McDonnell et al. 2025, Brain Stimulation; n=511 retrospective)**:
  - Associated with worse clinical response (CGI-I adjusted OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.05–2.44).
  - Associated with slightly faster reorientation (median 19.95 vs 22.65 min).
  - Associated with shorter EEG seizures only at the titration session (by 7.59 s).
  - Source: [Brain Stimulation 2025](https://www.brainstimjrnl.com/article/S1935-861X(25)00308-0/fulltext)
- **Anticonvulsants in bipolar disorder (Oliveira 2025 meta-analysis; 6 studies, n=359, mostly mania)**: higher thresholds (SMD 0.71) and shorter seizures (SMD −0.75), but no difference in symptom improvement — [Oliveira 2025](https://exa.ai/library/publication/fq716g93kg4)
- **Benzodiazepines (conflicting evidence)**:
  - Jha & Stein 1996 is titled "Decreased efficacy of combined benzodiazepines and unilateral ECT" (findings not retrieved).
  - Delamarre 2019 (n=70, titrated BT) found *higher* remission with benzodiazepines (81.2% vs 52.0%).
  - Source: [Delamarre 2019 and reference list](https://exa.ai/library/publication/vdnq360wr28)
- **Psychotropics and threshold (Chiao 2019, n=550)**: psychotropics, including benzodiazepines and antiepileptics, were not associated with titrated ST or seizure duration — [Chiao 2019](https://exa.ai/library/publication/7516tphdy57)

**Cognitive-protective adjuncts**
- **Verdijk et al. 2022 meta-analysis (Acta Psychiatr Scand 145:343–56)**:
  - Scope: 52 studies; 26 RCTs (n=1,387) meta-analysed.
  - Memantine: large effect, low-quality evidence. Short-term global cognition MD 0.73; immediate recall MD 1.10; n=78 across 2 studies.
  - Liothyronine (T3): medium effect, low-quality evidence. General memory SMD 0.73; 2 RCTs, n=50.
  - Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors: SMD 0.72 for short-term global cognition (4 studies, n=184), very low quality.
  - Ketamine, anti-inflammatory herbal preparations and opioid agonists showed no efficacy.
  - Source: [Verdijk 2022 full text](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/76212064/Acta_Psychiatr_Scand_2022_Verdijk_Pharmacological_interventions_to_diminish_cognitive_side_effects_of.pdf)
- **Andrade 2022 commentary**: 11 of the 26 RCTs were from Iran. "None of the RCTs examined clinically meaningful outcomes such as subjective cognitive impairment, impairments in daily life, and persistent autobiographical memory deficits"; "a risk is that whatever attenuates ECT-induced cognitive AEs may also attenuate ECT-related therapeutic benefits" — [J Clin Psychiatry 2022](https://www.psychiatrist.com/jcp/pharmacological-treatment-cognitive-adverse-effects-of-ect/)

### Inferences
- **Anaesthetic.** No agent has proven cognition-sparing effects. What matters is getting an adequate seizure without escalating electrical dose. Useful practices:
  - The lowest effective induction dose.
  - A properly long interval between induction and stimulus.
  - Remifentanil to allow a lower dose of the main agent.
  - Methohexital or etomidate instead of propofol if seizures are short.
- **Medication management:**
  - Minimise benzodiazepines and anticonvulsants before treatments.
  - Avoid lithium during the acute course, or keep levels low and hold the dose the day before.
  - Consider a concurrent tricyclic such as nortriptyline. Prior "failure" of the class does not negate its specific augmentation and cognitive data during ECT, and the same applies to continuation after ECT.
- **Memantine or T3** could be discussed as off-label options for a highly vulnerable patient, but the evidence is low quality, short-term and not clinically anchored.

### Gaps
- Ketofol (ketamine–propofol) and its effects on cognition were not specifically searched.
- Individual T3 trials (Stern 1991), donepezil and memantine trials (Alizadeh) were not retrieved individually; only the pooled data above.
- The findings of the Jha & Stein 1996 and Gálvez 2013 benzodiazepine papers were not retrieved.
- Matthews 2013 was not located.

## 7. Monitoring and adjustment: reorientation time, ECCA, MoCA and how good centres respond

### Takeaway
- **Reorientation:** time to reorientation after each session is the best early warning sign of later retrograde (autobiographical) amnesia. Baseline cognitive status is the main pre-treatment predictor.
- **Bedside tools:** the MoCA is insensitive to ECT-specific effects; it can even rise during a course. ECT-specific tools (ECCA, BECS) detect change.
- **Good centres** measure reorientation at every session, test cognition at baseline, every 3–4 treatments and the end of the course, and follow up at 1–2 months. They respond to warning signs by spacing treatments, switching to unilateral placement or shorter pulse width, or pausing.

### Cited Findings
- **Sobin et al. 1995 (Am J Psychiatry 152:995–1001), n=71**:
  - "Pre-ECT global cognitive status and the duration of postictal disorientation were strong predictors of the magnitude of retrograde amnesia in the week after the course of ECT and at 2-month follow-up", regardless of technique — [AJP](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/ajp.152.7.995)
  - Full text (OCR copy): at 2 months, among 28 single-course patients, retrograde amnesia correlated r≈0.46 with baseline modified MMSE and r≈0.47 with disorientation duration — [PDF copy](http://www.ectresources.org/ECTscience/Sobin__Sackeim__et_al__1995__Memory_worsened_by_prior_injury__bilater_worse_.pdf)
- **Martin, Gálvez & Loo 2015 (IJNP), n=74**:
  - The regression explained 32% of the variance in AMI-SF change, and time to reorientation after ECT 6 was the only significant predictor.
  - BT placement and longer pulse width were associated with longer reorientation at ECT 3.
  - Older age lengthened reorientation later in the course.
  - Source: [PMC4675978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4675978/)
- **RANZCP 2019 monitoring recommendations:**
  - "The routine monitoring of re-orientation is recommended, using a structured scale immediately following each ECT treatment"; scores should be reviewed ideally before each treatment.
  - Responses to emerging risk: "spacing ECT treatments further apart, or switching to a unilateral placement or shorter pulse width".
  - Brief ECT Cognitive Screen (BECS) before ECT and after the first week; MoCA before, during and after.
  - Neuropsychology referral if indicated; multiple seizures per session are "not acceptable practice".
  - Source: [RANZCP 2019](https://www.psychiatry.org/File%20Library/Psychiatrists/Practice/Clinical%20Practice%20Guidelines/ANZJP-Practice-Guidelines-for-ECT.pdf)
- **ECCA (Hermida et al. 2020, J Affect Disord 269:36–42)**:
  - 30 items, under 10 minutes. Domains: orientation, attention, delayed verbal recall, factual knowledge, autobiographical memory, plus subjective and informant ratings.
  - In 55 ECT patients, ECCA fell from 25.2 at baseline to 22.9 before the 6th session and 21.8 one week after ECT, while MoCA was 25.8 → 25.8 → 26.8.
  - Downloadable at fuquacenter.org/ecca.
  - Sources: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32217341/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032719331556)
- **Spanish ECCA (ECCA-S)**: sensitive to change across the course, whereas MMSE was not — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41671430/)
- **Dodd et al. 2025 systematic review**: of 9 studies, BECS and ECCA scored highest on pragmatic criteria, but there is "limited evidence to support the superiority of one cognitive tool over another" and no gold standard — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40953284/)
- **ECTAS (UK accreditation) 16th edition standards:**
  - Standardised cognitive assessment (e.g., ACE-III, MoCA) before the first treatment, after every 4 treatments, after the last session and at 2 months.
  - Subjective memory (CPRS item 17) before every treatment.
  - Access to neuropsychology.
  - Protocols on discontinuation for non-response and on seizure adequacy.
  - Consent must cover likelihood of success and cognitive risks.
  - Source: [ECTAS 16th ed.](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/improving-care/ccqi/quality-networks/electro-convulsive-therapy-clinics-(ectas)/ectas---16th-edition-standards.pdf?sfvrsn=84254f21_3)
- **Expectations and attribution (Swedish 8-hospital study, n=277)**:
  - Measured pre/post, subjective memory worsened in 16.2%, was unchanged in 52.3% and improved in 31.4%.
  - Yet 54.6% retrospectively said ECT harmed their memory.
  - Negative pre-ECT expectations predicted worsening (OR 1.83 per point), as did younger age.
  - Source: [BJPsych Open 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148217/)

### Inferences
- **Baseline tests:** the patient should get a documented baseline before ECT — ideally a neuropsychological battery that includes an autobiographical memory interview, processing speed and verbal learning. Because of the medical-school plans, the baseline should also include domain-relevant learned material.
- **Measures to request:**
  - Reorientation time recorded at every session with a structured scale.
  - An ECT-specific screen (ECCA or BECS) at baseline, around sessions 3–4 and 6–8, end of course and 1–2 months.
  - Explicit pre-agreed thresholds that trigger spacing, a switch or a pause.
- **Instrument choice:** a MoCA-only programme is likely to under-detect ECT-specific effects.

### Gaps
- No validated numeric reorientation cut-off (e.g., ">X minutes means change technique") was found; RANZCP leaves this to clinical review.
- Martin et al. 2018 (10-item Orientation Questionnaire) is cited in the NSW handbook, but its validation data were not retrieved.

## 8. Continuation and maintenance ECT: relapse and cumulative cognitive effects

### Takeaway
Relapse after successful ECT is high: about 38% by 6 months and 51% by 12 months despite continuation drug treatment. Continuation ECT alone is no better than optimised pharmacotherapy (CORE: 37% vs 32%). Combining symptom-titrated continuation ECT with medication beat medication alone in elderly UB-RUL remitters (PRIDE: relapse 13% vs 20%, HAM-D 4.2 points lower), with no MMSE difference. Data on cumulative cognitive effects of maintenance ECT are thin and mostly use insensitive measures.

### Cited Findings
- **Jelovac et al. 2013 meta-analysis (Neuropsychopharmacology), 32 studies**:
  - With modern continuation pharmacotherapy, relapse was 37.7% (95% CI 30.7–45.2) at 6 months and 51.1% (44.7–57.4) at 12 months.
  - Continuation ECT gave 37.2% relapse at 6 months; continuation ECT without medication gave 45.4%.
  - Antidepressants vs placebo halved 6-month relapse (RR 0.49, NNT 3.3), with the best evidence for tricyclics.
  - Source: [PMC3799066](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799066/)
- **Medication resistance and relapse**: the same review notes that established medication resistance predicts worse acute and longer-term ECT outcomes — [PMC3799066](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799066/)
- **CORE continuation trial (Kellner et al. 2006, Arch Gen Psychiatry 63:1337–44), n=201 bilateral-ECT remitters**:
  - Continuation ECT (10 treatments over 6 months): 37.1% relapsed, 46.1% stayed well, 16.8% dropped out.
  - Lithium plus nortriptyline: 31.6% relapsed, 46.3% stayed well, 22.1% dropped out.
  - Differences were not significant; half of relapses occurred within 4–6 weeks.
  - Source: [JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/209924)
- **PRIDE Phase 2 (Kellner et al. 2016, AJP 173:1110–18), n=120 elderly remitters after UB-RUL**:
  - Arms: 4 continuation ECTs in month 1, then ECT as needed by the STABLE algorithm plus venlafaxine and lithium, vs medication alone.
  - At 24 weeks, adjusted HAM-D was 4.2 points lower with ECT plus medication (95% CI 1.6–6.9).
  - Relapse was 13.1% (8/61) vs 20.3% (12/59); this was descriptive and the study was not powered for relapse.
  - "There was no statistically significant difference between groups in MMSE score".
  - Source: [PRIDE Phase 2](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16010118)
- **PRIDE neurocognition (Lisanby et al. 2020, Am J Geriatr Psychiatry)**: after the acute UB-RUL 6× ST course plus venlafaxine:
  - There were significant acute declines in processing speed, autobiographical-memory consistency (AMI-SF t = −22.1), short-term recall and recognition of words, phonemic fluency and TMT-B.
  - The magnitude was "clinically modest"; only phonemic fluency, complex visual scanning and cognitive flexibility moved from low-average to mildly impaired.
  - Verbal learning and delayed recall did not change.
  - The authors note that AMI-SF consistency falls over time even in depressed patients not receiving ECT.
  - Source: [PMC7050408](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7050408/)
- **Usage**: in the US 2022 survey, a median of 51% of patients received continuation or maintenance ECT — [US 2022 survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11136609/)

### Inferences
- **Plan relapse prevention before starting.** A 10-year TRD history with every drug class failed predicts high relapse risk.
- **Most evidence-supported strategy:** symptom-titrated continuation ECT combined with pharmacotherapy (the PRIDE model). Tricyclic-based or lithium-based continuation has the best drug evidence, but lithium raises cognitive and delirium concerns during active ECT.
- **Cognitive cost of maintenance:** use the most cognition-sparing technique (UB-RUL) with flexible, symptom-driven spacing rather than a fixed schedule.

### Gaps
- No rigorous long-term neuropsychological data were found on cumulative effects of years of maintenance UB-RUL in young adults. PRIDE used only the MMSE for Phase 2 cognition, and its subjects were ≥60.
- Kellner 2016 Phase 2 neurocognitive battery results were "reported elsewhere" and not retrieved.

## 9. Clinician and centre variability, and what to ask a clinic (checklist)

### Takeaway
There is direct evidence that *how* a centre delivers ECT drives cognitive outcomes:
- In seven New York hospitals, 6-month cognitive outcomes differed between sites, mostly explained by technique (sine wave, bilateral placement).
- Community remission rates (30–47%) fall far below trial rates, partly through premature stopping.
- Technique still varies by region: Europe rarely uses UB-RUL, BT is still common in Europe and Asia, and Sweden uses age-based dosing.

The suspicion that published side-effect rates are inflated by suboptimal practice is partly supported. However, even optimised UB-RUL produces measurable acute effects, and 19–26% of patients report subjective memory worsening in the week after treatment.

### Cited Findings
- **Sackeim et al. 2007 (Neuropsychopharmacology 32:244–54), 7 NYC-area hospitals, n=347**:
  - "The seven sites differed significantly in cognitive outcomes both immediately and 6 months following ECT, even when controlling for patient characteristics… differences in ECT technique largely accounted for these differences."
  - "Sine wave stimulation resulted in pronounced slowing of reaction time, both immediately and 6 months following ECT."
  - Bilateral ECT gave more severe and persistent retrograde amnesia.
  - Risk factors were older age, lower premorbid IQ and female sex.
  - Source: [Nature/NPP](https://www.nature.com/articles/1301180)
  - Full text: "only receipt of BL treatment distinguished the group with marked and persistent retrograde amnesia"; patients treated only with BL showed 2.8× the forgetting of healthy controls at 6 months — [Hosted copy of paper](https://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/23.-Sackeim-H.-et-al.2007.-The-cognitive-effects-of-ECT-in-community-settings.pdf)
- **Prudic et al. 2004**: same community sample. Remission was 30.3–46.7%, relapse among remitters 64.3%, and courses were often ended with significant residual symptoms — [Biol Psychiatry](https://www.biologicalpsychiatryjournal.com/article/S0006-3223(03)01046-1/abstract)
- **Site effects in a controlled trial (Sackeim 2009, OPT-ECT, 3 university sites)**: significant site effects on both remission and cognition. Remission was higher at one site (WPIC) than another (WU); cognitive deficits were greatest at a third (WF) — [JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/483117)
- **International variation (2022 survey, 126 sites)**:
  - Sites pairing UB with RUL: US 74%, Europe 13%; Europe favoured 0.5 ms (44%).
  - For depressive episodes, 56% of sites prefer RUL and 20% prefer BT.
  - 80% use quantitative cognitive outcome measures.
  - Source: [Rohde et al. 2023/24](https://exa.ai/library/publication/yl7gv657xgh); [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1612556)
- **US survey 2022 (74 sites)**: 66% prefer RUL for depressive episodes; 84% use quantitative cognitive measures; 85% treat thrice weekly; median course 10 — [PMC11136609](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11136609/)
- **Australia (CARE Network, 18 sites)**: substantial heterogeneity; RUL (44%) and BF (39%) most common; 81% use individual titration. In a 2007 Australian survey, 38% used fixed-dose methods. Recent European surveys show dominant BT — [CARE Network 2017](https://exa.ai/library/publication/0nmzq0v0csm)
- **Placement worldwide**: "brief pulse bitemporal ECT remains the most common form of ECT worldwide" (2017) — [Kolshus 2017](https://www.stpatricks.ie/media/1839/bitemporal-v-high-dose-right-unilateral-electroconvulsive-therapy-for-depression-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-randomized-controlled-trials.pdf)
- **Accreditation frameworks**:
  - ECTAS (UK and Ireland; established 2003) requires 100% of Type 1, 80% of Type 2 and 60% of Type 3 standards — [ECTAS 16th ed.](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/improving-care/ccqi/quality-networks/electro-convulsive-therapy-clinics-(ectas)/ectas---16th-edition-standards.pdf?sfvrsn=84254f21_3)
  - SEAN (Scotland, formed 1996) is cited as a model network — [US 2022 survey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11136609/)
- **Persistent effects with modern technique (Swedish Q-ECT 2024 annual report)**:
  - 46% reached remission (MADRS ≤10) at the end of the index series.
  - 19% reported memory worsening of ≥2 steps within a week, while 21% reported a corresponding improvement.
  - Source: [Q-ECT Årsrapport 2024 (Swedish)](https://ect.rcnorr.se/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Kvalitetsregister-ECT-Arsrapport-2024_final_web.pdf)

### Inferences
**Patient checklist for judging a clinic.** Each item is derived from the evidence and guidelines cited above.
1. **Accreditation and audit.** Is the clinic accredited (ECTAS/SEAN in the UK; CARE-network participation in Australia; for US clinics, ask about audit and outcome registries)? Does it publish or share its own remission and cognitive outcome data?
2. **Dosing method.** Do you titrate seizure threshold at the first session (not age-based or fixed dose)? What multiple will I get? Expect about 6× for RUL (6–8× if UB) and 1.5× for BT.
3. **Placement.** What is your default placement for non-urgent TRD? Expect high-dose RUL. Under what conditions do you switch to BF or BT, and do you re-titrate when switching?
4. **Pulse width.** What pulse width do you use with RUL? Do you offer 0.25–0.3 ms? Do you avoid ultrabrief with bilateral placements? Do you use sine wave? The answer must be no.
5. **Frequency.** Twice or thrice weekly? Can I move to twice weekly or space treatments if cognitive signals emerge?
6. **Outcome tracking and stopping.**
   - Do you rate depression with MADRS or HAM-D every 1–2 sessions?
   - What are the pre-defined remission and plateau criteria for stopping?
   - What is the switch point (6–8 sessions for UB; 4–6 for BP)?
   - Is there a second-opinion review at 12 sessions?
7. **Cognitive monitoring.**
   - Do you record time to reorientation after every session?
   - Which tool do you use: ECCA, BECS or MoCA? How often (baseline, every 3–4 sessions, end, 1–2 months)?
   - What do you do if reorientation lengthens?
   - Is neuropsychology referral available?
8. **Anaesthesia.**
   - Which agent? What is your anaesthetic-to-stimulus interval?
   - Do you use remifentanil to minimise induction dose?
   - Do you monitor seizure quality on EEG (amplitude, post-ictal suppression), not just duration?
9. **Medication plan.**
   - Benzodiazepines and anticonvulsants: taper or hold before sessions.
   - Lithium: hold or reduce.
   - Augment with nortriptyline rather than venlafaxine if an antidepressant is co-prescribed.
10. **Continuation plan.** Symptom-titrated continuation ECT plus pharmacotherapy; how will maintenance spacing be adjusted?
11. **Clinic experience.** Annual volume and experience with UB-RUL and with switching protocols.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares outcomes of accredited vs non-accredited clinics.
- APA Task Force 2001 primary text, NICE guidance and ISEN standards were not retrieved; APA content is from secondary summaries.
- The Royal College ECT Handbook (4th edition) was not retrieved.

## 10. State-of-the-art "cognition-sparing" protocol and expected numbers for TRD

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced protocol that maximises response while keeping cognitive cost low is:
- RUL (d'Elia) with ST titrated at session 1, starting ultrabrief (0.25–0.3 ms) at 6–8× ST.
- Thrice weekly, or twice weekly if speed is not critical.
- Pre-defined escalation: UB → brief-pulse RUL (0.5–1.0 ms, 5–6× ST) → BF or BT at 1.5× ST.
- Stop at remission or plateau.
- Every-session reorientation monitoring plus an ECT-specific cognitive screen.
- Minimise anticonvulsants, benzodiazepines and lithium; consider nortriptyline augmentation.
- Planned continuation (symptom-titrated continuation ECT plus medication).

Expected remission ranges from about 46–73% in trials to about 48% in medication-resistant cohorts and 30–47% in community practice. Patient factors in this case (young age, 10-year episode, all classes failed) point toward the lower end. Relapse is about 25–38% at 6 months even with continuation treatment.

### Cited Findings
- **UB-RUL 6× ST, thrice weekly**: 73% remission (n=22), with no cognitive measure deteriorating relative to baseline — [Sackeim 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742986/)
- **PRIDE (age ≥60, plus venlafaxine)**: 61.7% remission and 28.3% dropout — [PRIDE Phase 1](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15081101)
- **UB-RUL 8× ST**: efficacy comparable to BP-RUL 5× ST, with less impairment of orientation and autobiographical memory — [Loo 2015](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/18/1/pyu045/651640)
- **Pragmatic twice-weekly high-dose RUL (EFFECT-Dep)**: remission 46.4% and 6-month relapse 25% — [EFFECT-Dep](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15030372)
- **Medication-resistant patients**: remission 48.0% (281/585) vs 64.9% (242/373) without prior pharmacotherapy failure (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.39–0.69; observational) — [Heijnen 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20814336/)
- **Community practice**: remission 30.3–46.7%. Longer episode duration and comorbid personality disorder predicted poorer outcome — [Prudic 2004](https://www.biologicalpsychiatryjournal.com/article/S0006-3223(03)01046-1/abstract)
- **Young age**: in Swedish RUL patients, self-rated remission 6 months after ECT was 18.6% at ages 16–39 vs 62.2% at ≥65 — [PMC7676462](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7676462/)
- **Relapse**: 37.7% at 6 months and 51.1% at 12 months with continuation pharmacotherapy — [Jelovac 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799066/)
- **Concurrent nortriptyline**: about 15 percentage points higher remission and fewer cognitive effects — [Sackeim 2009](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/483117)
- **Recovery of objective cognition (Semkovska & McLoughlin 2010 meta-analysis; 84 studies, 2,981 patients)**:
  - 72% of cognitive variables were significantly reduced 0–3 days after ECT (effect sizes −1.10 to −0.21).
  - By 4–15 days, all but one variable had recovered to baseline or better.
  - After 15 days there were no negative effects, and 57% of variables were above baseline (ES 0.35–0.75), including processing speed and anterograde memory.
  - No standardised retrograde amnesia tests were included.
  - Source: [Semkovska 2010](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/objective-cognitive-performance-associated-with-electroconvulsive/)
- **RANZCP**: ECT "can cause retrograde amnesia (including for autobiographical memories) that can be persistent… [risk] cannot always be predicted beforehand" — [RANZCP 2019](https://www.psychiatry.org/File%20Library/Psychiatrists/Practice/Clinical%20Practice%20Guidelines/ANZJP-Practice-Guidelines-for-ECT.pdf)

### Inferences
**Parameter-by-parameter evidence map.** All entries are summarised from the findings above.

| Factor | Cognition-sparing choice | Effect on cognition (acute / persistent) | Effect on efficacy and speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Placement | RUL (d'Elia) at high dose | Reorientation 8.3 min faster; autobiographical memory g = −0.46; BT amnesia still worse at 6 months (EFFECT-Dep OR 0.59) | Remission RR 1.06 (ns); BT faster in sessions 2–6 |
| Pulse width | 0.25–0.3 ms (RUL only) | Less impairment in all domains (Tor 2015); reorientation +108% with BP-RUL vs UB-RUL; autobiographical advantage to 6 months in one RCT, none at 6 months in another | SMD 0.25 favours BP; remission OR 0.71 (NNT about 12); about 1–2 extra sessions; UB-BL clearly inferior (35%) |
| Dose | RUL 6× (UB 6–8×); BT 1.5× | Higher multiple = more acute cognitive impact; no dose effect after 3 days for RUL | RUL needs ≥5–6× (17% at 1×, 43% at 2.5×); BT 2.5× is not better than 1.5× |
| Dosing method | Titration at session 1 | Avoids over-dosing (age-based dosing is excessive in 30% of women; up to 17× ST) | Avoids under-dosing (half-age ineffective in up to 64%) |
| Frequency | 2× weekly (or space when signals appear) | Less memory impairment (Lerer, Shapira, McAllister) | Same final outcome; about 5–6 extra days; 1× weekly is inferior |
| Session count | Stop at remission or plateau; switch at 4–6 (BP) or 6–8 (UB) sessions | BL amnesia rises linearly with number of sessions to 6 months; RUL/BF sessions do not | Premature stopping lowers remission (community 30–47%) |
| Anaesthesia | Lowest effective dose; adequate induction-to-stimulus interval; remifentanil-sparing | No agent proven cognition-sparing; ketamine no benefit | Propofol shortens seizures; longer interval improves seizure quality |
| Concurrent drugs | Nortriptyline yes; avoid lithium, benzodiazepines and anticonvulsants where possible | Nortriptyline improves cognition; venlafaxine worsens it; lithium raises delirium odds about 12-fold | Nortriptyline +15 points remission; anticonvulsants worsen response (OR 1.60) |
| Protective drugs | Memantine or T3 (off-label, low-quality evidence) | Short-term medium–large effects; no data on persistent memory | Unknown; theoretical risk of blunting efficacy |
| Monitoring | Reorientation every session; ECCA/BECS at baseline, every 3–4 sessions, end, 1–2 months | Early reorientation time is the strongest predictor of later amnesia | Measurement-based stopping and switching |
| Continuation | Symptom-titrated continuation ECT plus medication (PRIDE) | No MMSE difference vs medication alone (elderly) | Relapse 13% vs 20% at 6 months; HAM-D −4.2 |

**Realistic expectations for this patient (inference).**
- Achievable remission with a well-run protocol is plausibly about 45–60%, because medication resistance, a long episode and young age each predict lower response than the 61–73% seen in older or less resistant trial samples.
- Escalating to BP-RUL and then BT recovers some non-responders: 48% of RUL-to-BT crossovers remitted.
- Acute cognitive effects during the course are near-universal but mostly resolve within about 2 weeks.
- The residual risk of persistent autobiographical gaps is lowest with UB-RUL, but no published protocol makes it zero.
- Timing ECT so it does not overlap with intensive coursework, plus 2–4 weeks of recovery afterwards, is a practical mitigation.

**Is the "inflated by suboptimal practice" hypothesis right?** Partly.
- *Supported:* sine-wave stimulation, bilateral default placement, fixed or formula dosing, thrice-weekly default, prolonged courses and lithium co-administration all demonstrably worsen cognition, and all persist in parts of the world.
- *Not fully supported:* even optimal UB-RUL produces statistically detectable acute declines (PRIDE). Subjective memory worsening in modern Swedish practice (mostly 0.5 ms RUL) is 19–26% in the first week and about 16% at about 2–3 months.

### Gaps
- No trial tests a full "bundle" (UB-RUL, titration, twice weekly, reorientation-guided spacing and medication optimisation) against usual care.
- No outcome data were found specifically for young adults with ≥10-year chronic TRD who have failed all classes.
- Magnetic seizure therapy, FEAT and other novel seizure therapies are excluded here; another researcher covers them.
- Long-term (>6 months) objective autobiographical-memory outcomes for UB-RUL in young adults are lacking.
