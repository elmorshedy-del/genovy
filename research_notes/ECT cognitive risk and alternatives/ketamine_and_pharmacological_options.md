# Ketamine/Esketamine vs ECT, and Novel / Under-used Pharmacological Options for TRD (status as of October 2026)

Research note scope: head-to-head and indirect ketamine/esketamine vs ECT evidence, cognitive profiles, ketamine-with-ECT, and a scan of pharmacological, psychedelic, pipeline and adjunctive options. Neuromodulation devices other than ECT-as-comparator are out of scope. Patient context used for relevance: young adult, about 10 years of TRD, outpatient, reports failing "all classes" of antidepressants with no meaningful benefit, sensitive to psychomotor slowing and brain fog, rTMS failed, planning medical school.

Method note: Tavily and Exa were the main search and extract tools, and ClinicalTrials.gov was used for registry checks. Scite (mcp__Scite__search_literature) was loaded but returned "MCP tools require a paid scite plan or an active free trial," so no claims were checked through Scite. Peer-reviewed claims were checked against the journal page, PubMed/PMC or the publisher PDF wherever possible. Claims supported only by a press release or company filing are marked **[PRESS RELEASE ONLY]**.

## 1. Head-to-head ketamine vs ECT: trials, meta-analyses, durability, and who does better with which

### Takeaway
Two adequately powered randomized trials point in opposite directions. ELEKT-D (n=403, mostly outpatients, no psychosis, 3-week course) found ketamine noninferior and numerically better (response 55.4% vs 41.2%). KetECT (n=186 inpatients, up to 12 sessions) found ECT superior (remission 63% vs 46%). Pooled analyses that weight inpatients or adjust for time favor ECT, with a moderate efficacy advantage and faster improvement. ELEKT-D's ECT arm is widely criticised: 169 of 170 started on ultrabrief right-unilateral ECT, the course was short, and pre-treatment dropout was high. Durability is poor for both treatments without maintenance therapy. Ketamine looks like the better first choice for moderately severe, non-psychotic outpatients. ECT keeps the edge for very severe, inpatient and (probably) psychotic presentations.

### Cited Findings

**ELEKT-D (Anand et al., NEJM 2023;388:2315; NCT03113968)**
- Design: open-label, randomized noninferiority trial at 5 US sites, in patients referred to ECT clinics with nonpsychotic TRD. ECT 3 times per week vs IV ketamine 0.5 mg/kg over 40 min twice per week, for 3 weeks. Primary outcome was response (≥50% drop in QIDS-SR16). Noninferiority margin −10 percentage points — [NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2302399)
- 403 randomized (200 ketamine, 203 ECT). 38 withdrew before starting treatment, leaving 195 who received ketamine and 170 who received ECT (so 33 ECT-assigned vs 5 ketamine-assigned patients never started, from 203−170 and 200−195). Response was 55.4% (108/195) with ketamine vs 41.2% (70/170) with ECT: difference 14.2 points, 95% CI 3.9–24.2, P<0.001 for noninferiority. The ITT multiple-imputation sensitivity analysis gave 55.4% vs 41.6% — [NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2302399)
- Memory: change in HVLT-R delayed-recall T-score was −0.9±1.1 with ketamine vs −9.7±1.2 with ECT (difference 8.8, 95% CI 5.7–11.9). Total recall +3.2 vs −2.1. SMCQ 0.2 vs −8.8. Self-rated memory (GSE-My) 4.2 vs 3.2 — [NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2302399)
- Memory recovery: HVLT-R scores in the ECT group were back to levels similar to ketamine by the 1-month follow-up and stayed stable to 6 months. Self-rated memory (GSE-My) in the ECT group stayed lower than in the ketamine group through the end of follow-up — [NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2302399)
- Durability among responders, with treatment left to the clinical provider: relapse (QIDS-SR16 >11) was 19.0% ketamine vs 35.4% ECT at month 1, 25.0% vs 50.9% at month 3, and 34.5% vs 56.3% at month 6. Quality-of-life gains were similar in both arms (+12.3 vs +12.9) — [NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2302399)
- Adverse effects: ECT was associated with musculoskeletal effects and ketamine with dissociation — [NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2302399). PCORI summary: at least one moderate/severe AE in 25% (ketamine) vs 32% (ECT), not significant — [PCORI](https://www.pcori.org/research-results/2016/comparing-ketamine-versus-electroconvulsive-therapy-patients-treatment-resistant-depression-elekt-d-study)
- Remission by MADRS was 37.9% (74/195) ketamine vs 21.8% (37/170) ECT. MADRS response was 50.8% vs 41.1% — [Ekstrand et al., IJNP 2024 commentary](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/27/1/pyad065/7479950)

**Criticisms of the ELEKT-D ECT arm**
- Almost all ECT participants in ELEKT-D (169 of 170 in the modified ITT sample) started on ultrabrief-pulse high-dose right unilateral (RUL) ECT. In a 2025 meta-analysis, this ECT form had pooled remission of 32.2% (95% CI 26.1–39.0%) and response of 45.3% (39.0–51.7%), and 28.1% of patients were switched to brief-pulse ECT for inadequate response — [Molecular Psychiatry 2025 meta-analysis of ultrabrief-pulse ECT](https://preview-www.nature.com/articles/s41380-025-03327-z.pdf)
- KetECT investigators and ECT experts (Ekstrand, Takamiya, Nordenskjöld, Kirov, Sienaert) called ELEKT-D's 22% ECT remission rate "a clear outlier, whether community based or not." They contrasted the primary endpoints (ELEKT-D: self-reported QIDS-SR16 response, set by the funder; KetECT: clinician-rated MADRS remission). They also contrasted the noninferiority assumptions (ELEKT-D: a presumed 50% ECT response with a 10-point margin) — [IJNP 2024](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/27/1/pyad065/7479950)
- PCORI's own summary flags that both treatments lasted only 3 weeks and "results may have differed with longer treatment" — [PCORI](https://www.pcori.org/research-results/2016/comparing-ketamine-versus-electroconvulsive-therapy-patients-treatment-resistant-depression-elekt-d-study)
- Contrast: in an RCT by Sackeim et al. (n=90), ultrabrief RUL ECT at 6× seizure threshold reached 73% remission with the fewest cognitive effects. Ultrabrief bilateral ECT reached only 35% — [Sackeim et al., Brain Stimulation 2008 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2742986/)

**KetECT (Ekstrand et al., Int J Neuropsychopharmacol 2022;25(5):339–349; NCT02659085)**
- Hospitalized unipolar depression (age 18–85, MADRS ≥20). Ketamine 0.5 mg/kg or ECT, each 3 times per week, up to 12 sessions. Remission was 63% (57/91) with ECT vs 46% (44/95) with ketamine (P=.026; 95% CI of difference 2–30%). ITT: 61% vs 45% — [Ekstrand et al. 2022](https://exa.ai/library/publication/7jtjk0zbyv3); [DOI 10.1093/ijnp/pyab088](http://dx.doi.org/10.1093/ijnp/pyab088)
- Both arms needed a median of 6 sessions to remit. Fast single-infusion remission was rare with ketamine. Serious and long-lasting AEs, including persisting amnesia, were more common with ECT ("a handful" still had amnesia at 12 months). Ketamine AEs caused more dropouts — [Ekstrand et al. 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35020871)
- 12-month relapse among remitters was 70% (31/44) with ketamine vs 63–64% (36/56) with ECT (log-rank P=.44; median 57 vs 61 days in remission) — [Ekstrand et al. 2022](https://exa.ai/library/publication/7jtjk0zbyv3); [Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/967472)
- 22% of ketamine-arm patients dropped out early (before the 6th treatment) vs 4% of ECT, mainly from dissociative discomfort. Among non-psychotic patients, remission was 60% (46/77) ECT vs 45% (35/77) ketamine. In psychotic depression, ketamine remission was 50% (9/18) — [IJNP 2024 commentary](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/27/1/pyad065/7479950)

**Meta-analyses**
- Rhee et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2022;79(12):1162: 6 trials, 340 patients, all inpatient, 5 RCTs, ELEKT-D not included. Pooled SMD favored ECT at −0.69 (95% CI −0.89 to −0.48; I²=39%). No significant differences in cognition/memory or serious AEs — [PMC9582972](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9582972)
- Petrucci et al., Psychiatry Res 2024;337:115994: 6 RCTs, pooled n=655, ELEKT-D included. Ketamine noninferiority was not shown overall. Response RR 0.96 (0.70–1.31) and remission RR 0.91 (0.45–1.82) were not significant, with heavy heterogeneity (I² 75–90%). Among inpatients, ECT was superior for response, remission and score change. Ketamine gave better post-treatment cognition and less muscle pain but more dissociation, blurred vision, dizziness and diplopia. Relapse rates were similar — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165178124002798); [PMC clinician guide summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11995899) (via search snippet)
- Nikolin, Massaneda-Tuneu, Brettell … Loo, Transl Psychiatry 2026 ("Time matters for metas"): 7 studies, 731 participants. It reports that 8 earlier meta-analyses (all since 2022) disagreed. After modelling time and adjusting for baseline, ECT improved faster by about 0.02 SMD/day (β=0.018, p<0.0001), predicting SMD 0.59 (95% CI −0.26 to 1.43) in favor of ECT by 4 weeks. Baseline depression was lower in the ketamine groups (SMD −0.28) — [Nature Transl Psychiatry](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-026-03806-z)
- A 2025–26 meta-analysis in a lower-tier journal (18 studies; 5 with response data, n=760) found no difference in response (RR 1.13, 0.85–1.49) or remission. Leaving out ELEKT-D gave a significant ECT advantage (RR 1.27, 1.07–1.51) and resolved most heterogeneity — [Int J Med Sci Health Res](https://internationalmedicaljournal.org/index.php/ijmhsr/article/view/724). Lower-quality venue; treat as corroborative only.
- An expression of concern (Jelovac, Nikolin, Loo, McLoughlin, J Affect Disord 2025) was issued about the Shi et al. 2024 ketamine-vs-ECT meta-analysis. That meta-analysis should not be relied on — [reference list in Int J Med Sci Health Res](https://internationalmedicaljournal.org/index.php/ijmhsr/article/view/724)

**Who does better with which (ELEKT-D secondary analysis; Jha et al., JAMA Netw Open 2024)**
- Outpatients improved more on QIDS-SR16 with ketamine than ECT (−8.4 vs −6.2), as did patients with baseline QIDS-SR16 ≤20 (−7.7 vs −5.6). Inpatients improved more with ECT (−10.9 vs −8.0). Very severe patients (QIDS-SR16 >20) improved faster with ECT early on, but end-of-treatment scores were similar (−9.0 vs −9.9) — [JAMA Netw Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2820305)
- Exploratory findings, not significant after FDR correction: with premorbid IQ estimate (NAART-35) <85, response was 42.7% ketamine vs 20.3% ECT. In patients taking an atypical antipsychotic, MADRS remission was 42.9% ketamine vs 10.6% ECT. Within the ECT arm, higher premorbid IQ and comorbid PTSD predicted larger MADRS reductions — [JAMA Netw Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2820305)
- The authors conclude that outpatients with nonpsychotic TRD of moderately severe or severe depression "may consider ketamine over ECT" — [PubMed 38916891](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38916891/)
- ELEKT-D excluded psychotic depression — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03113968](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03113968)

**Other ketamine-route evidence relevant to comparisons**
- KADS (Loo et al., BJPsych 2023): phase 3 trial of subcutaneous racemic ketamine twice weekly for 4 weeks vs midazolam. Fixed 0.5 mg/kg was no better than control (remission 6.3% vs 8.8%). Flexible 0.5–0.9 mg/kg gave remission of 19.6% vs 2.0% (OR 12.1; NNT about 6) and response of 29% vs 4%. Remission fell to 8.0% vs 2.1% four weeks after treatment stopped. 24% of the sample had previously failed ECT — [BJPsych / PMC10727911](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10727911/)

### Inferences
- The patient fits ELEKT-D's "ketamine-favoring" profile: young, outpatient, non-psychotic, and (presumably) moderately severe to severe rather than catatonic or psychotic. ELEKT-D's ECT arm probably underestimates what optimized ECT can do (brief-pulse or bilateral, more than 9 sessions, switching non-responders). So "ketamine ≥ ECT" should not be generalised. The fairer statement: ketamine gives a reasonable chance of response with a clearly better short-term cognitive profile, and ECT has the higher efficacy ceiling.
- With a 10-year illness, chronicity is a known negative prognostic factor for ECT. The KetECT authors note that ELEKT-D's low ECT response is linked to factors including prolonged episode duration ([IJNP 2024](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/27/1/pyad065/7479950)). Expected ECT remission for this patient is therefore probably below the 60–70% figures quoted for inpatient samples.
- Both treatments need a maintenance plan. Untreated relapse after either acute course runs at about 50–70% within 6–12 months.
- A pragmatic sequence consistent with the evidence: an adequate ketamine or esketamine trial first. Ketamine non-response should not be read as predicting ECT non-response, since the mechanisms are distinct and KetECT showed ECT outperforming ketamine in hard cases. ECT remains the fallback.

### Gaps
- I could not get full text of the KetECT/ELEKT-D comparison commentary (OUP fetch failed) or the original NEJM letters criticising ELEKT-D. Exact pulse width and switching rules for the ELEKT-D ECT arm come from the Molecular Psychiatry meta-analysis statement, not the NEJM supplement.
- Kheirabadi 2019, Ghasemi 2014 and Allen 2015 were not individually retrieved. They are among the small inpatient RCTs pooled in Rhee 2022 and Petrucci 2024, so their individual effect sizes are not reported here.
- "Menon 2023" meta-analysis was not located. The Moreira 2023 (J Affect Disord) meta-analysis was identified by citation only.
- No head-to-head trial of esketamine nasal spray vs ECT was found. Nikolin 2026 states that none was identified.

## 2. Cognitive effects and long-term safety of ketamine and esketamine (including maintenance data)

### Takeaway
Repeated clinical-dose ketamine has a neutral-to-favorable cognitive profile once the acute 1–2 hour dissociative window has passed. A 2026 systematic review (21 studies) found no cognitive deterioration, and processing speed improved in 13 of 15 studies. Head-to-head, ketamine beat ECT on cognition at 3 weeks, with no difference among responders by 1–6 months. Esketamine's 6.5-year open-label extension (SUSTAIN-3, 1,148 patients, 3,777 patient-years) showed stable cognition, no cystitis or hepatotoxicity signal, and about half the patients in remission on intermittent dosing. A slight reaction-time slowing appeared mainly in patients 65 and older. Real harms (memory deficits, ulcerative cystitis, dependence) are documented in heavy recreational users at far higher frequency and dose.

### Cited Findings

**Repeated-dose clinical ketamine and cognition**
- ELEKT-D cognitive secondary analysis (Kumpf et al. 2025): after a 3-week course, ketamine patients had better cognitive functioning than ECT patients, with no group differences among responders at 1, 3 or 6 months. Within the ketamine group, executive function and cognitive flexibility improved, and this held after adjusting for mood change. "Findings support the short-term superiority of ketamine on cognitive functioning and the long-term cognitive safety of both treatments" — [PubMed 40900112](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40900112/)
- Chavigny et al., Psychological Medicine 2026, systematic review: 21 studies, about 900–1,180 TRD participants, IV ketamine. Processing speed improved in 13 of 15 studies, often persisting up to 4 weeks after the last infusion and partly independent of mood. Working memory improved in 9 of 16. Verbal and visual memory results were mixed. "No cognitive deterioration was reported" — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/cognitive-effects-of-intravenous-ketamine-in-treatmentresistant-depression-a-systematic-review/171BAC6B88358C98C68E9CE49A34DA01)
- Shiroma et al., IJNP 2014 (n=15; six 0.5 mg/kg infusions over 12 days): visual memory, simple working memory and complex working memory improved after the last infusion, with no short-term impairment at 4 weeks. Gains were explained by mood improvement. Lower baseline attention predicted response — [IJNP 2014](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/17/11/1805/2910063)
- Zavaliangos-Petropulu et al., J Affect Disord 2023 (n=66, four 0.5 mg/kg infusions): inhibition, working memory, processing speed and fluid cognition improved. Processing speed and fluid cognition gains lasted through 5 weeks and were statistically independent of antidepressant response — [PubMed 37060953](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37060953/)
- Bio-K multicenter trial (n=74): 53% (39/74) remitted after 3 IV infusions. RBANS immediate and delayed memory, language and attention improved, and gains persisted in the continuation phase — [Psychiatry Res 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165178124001148)
- Naturalistic chart review (n=22, 8–10 escalating-dose infusions, 0.5 up to 1.0 mg/kg): MoCA 26.77 before vs 26.68 after, no impairment. The Ning group (six 0.5 mg/kg infusions over 12 days) found no cognitive deterioration — [Dai et al., BMC Psychiatry 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12888-022-03789-3)
- Acute effects: one study found selective delayed-recall impairment 40 min after a single infusion (Murrough 2013), as cited in [Shiroma 2014](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/17/11/1805/2910063). In healthy volunteers, single infusions of 0.4–0.8 mg/kg dose-dependently impair episodic and working memory — [Dai et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12888-022-03789-3)
- Gill et al. 2020 review: subanesthetic IV ketamine "does not worsen cognitive function" in TRD — [Neurosci Biobehav Rev](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763420306497)

**Esketamine long-term (SUSTAIN-3) and comparative (ESCAPE-TRD)**
- SUSTAIN-3 final (Zaki et al., IJNP 2025;28(6)): 1,148 adults, up to 6.5 years, 3,777 patient-years, mean exposure 42.9 months. Remission was 35.6% at the end of induction and 48.5–49.6% at week 112 and the maintenance endpoint. Discontinuation was 5.3% for lack of efficacy and 6.4% for AEs. Common AEs: headache 36.9%, dizziness 33.9%, nausea 33.6%, dissociation 25.5%, somnolence 23.1%. 97% of dosing-day AEs resolved the same day. No new BP, renal, urinary, suicidality, cognition, cystitis or hepatotoxicity concerns. No evidence of abuse, misuse or withdrawal under the supervised delivery model — [Zaki et al. 2025](https://exa.ai/library/publication/4yk26lgzwvt); [Imperial repository](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/a3adba8e-3d34-47a5-a37b-6a7137ab0c05)
- SUSTAIN-3 interim (Neuropsychopharmacology 2023): no cognitive decline in patients under 65 through week 160, with numerical improvements in higher cognition (z +0.11 to +0.28). Choice reaction time slowed slightly (z −0.22) under 65 and more in those 65 and older (z −0.37). The clinical significance is unclear with no control arm — [Nature NPP](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41386-023-01577-5)
- SUSTAIN-3, US-label subgroup aged 18–64: elevated BP AEs in 14.5% of patients, 96.4% of which resolved the same day. 3.3% needed BP medication. 0.5% had a cognition-related AE — [J&J NEI 2024 poster](https://www.jnjmedicalconnect.com/media/attestation/congresses/neuroscience/2024/nei/longterm-safety-of-esketamine-nasal-spray-dosed-in-accordance-with-us-prescribing-information-in-adu.pdf). Industry poster.
- ESCAPE-TRD (Reif et al., NEJM 2023): 676 patients, esketamine nasal spray vs quetiapine XR, both added to an SSRI/SNRI. Week-8 remission (MADRS ≤10) was 27.1% vs 17.6% (P=0.003; aOR 1.74). Relapse-free remission through week 32 was 21.7% vs 14.1%. Week-32 remission was 49.1% vs 32.9% (NRI) — [NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2304145)
- ESCAPE-TRD discontinuations from AEs: esketamine dizziness 0.6%, dissociation 0.6%, vomiting 0.6%. Quetiapine XR sedation 2.1%, weight gain 1.8%, somnolence 1.5% — [European Psychiatry abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-psychiatry/article/esketamine-nasal-spray-shows-higher-remission-and-response-rates-over-32-weeks-of-treatment-compared-with-quetiapine-extendedrelease-in-patients-with-treatment-resistant-depression-results-from-escapetrd-a-randomised-phase-iiib-clinical-trial/F7BAD645749E43865CB8815D8BED9559)
- Regulatory: the FDA approved Spravato as **monotherapy** for TRD in adults (approval letter 17 Jan 2025, announced 21 Jan 2025) — [J&J press release](https://www.jnj.com/media-center/press-releases/spravato-esketamine-approved-in-the-u-s-as-the-first-and-only-monotherapy-for-adults-with-treatment-resistant-depression). Per a clinic source, the REMS, at least 2 hours of post-dose monitoring and the no-driving rule are unchanged — [MindVibe](https://www.mindvibe.com/blog/spravato-without-antidepressant-monotherapy) (secondary source). A further J&J phase 3 monotherapy RCT (NCT07716098, n=348) is recruiting — [ClinicalTrials.gov search](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07716098)

**Recreational and heavy-use harms (context for clinical risk)**
- Morgan, Muetzelfeldt & Curran, Addiction 2010 (1-year longitudinal study, n=150): cognitive deficits were mainly confined to frequent users, who averaged about 20 days/month of use in the cohort's earlier study. Rising use correlated with worse spatial working memory and pattern-recognition memory. Frequent users had more dissociative and delusional symptoms. Abstinent ex-users showed no deficits vs controls, suggesting possible recovery — [Addiction 2010](https://www.ovid.com/19919593.pmid); [full text PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychedelic/ketamine/2009-morgan.pdf)
- Morgan & Curran, Addiction 2012 review: ketamine-induced ulcerative cystitis is "particularly associated with chronic, frequent use." Frequent daily use is linked to working and episodic memory deficits. Many frequent users report failed attempts to stop — [PubMed 21777321](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21777321/)
- About 20% of frequent recreational users report cystitis-like symptoms vs 6.7% of infrequent users. Regular use raises cystitis-symptom risk 3–4-fold, and stopping usually improves symptoms — [Ketamine-induced cystitis review, PMC9476224](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9476224/)
- Surveillance data (DART, cited in SUSTAIN-3) show a rising trend in misuse/abuse of ketamine but not of esketamine — [Zaki et al. 2025](https://exa.ai/library/publication/4yk26lgzwvt)

### Inferences
- For a side-effect-sensitive future medical student, ketamine-class treatment carries a time-limited cognitive cost: about 1–2 hours of dissociation on each dosing day, plus no driving. There is no signal of cumulative cognitive harm at clinical doses up to about 6.5 years (esketamine), and processing speed may improve. That is a better match to "brain fog/psychomotor slowing" concerns than ECT or sedating augmenters such as quetiapine.
- The main long-term risks are needing indefinite maintenance (SUSTAIN-3 used weekly or every-other-week dosing for most patients), BP spikes, and misuse liability if racemic ketamine is used unsupervised (for example take-home oral or lozenge ketamine). Supervised esketamine has the best long-term safety dataset.
- Dissociation-driven dropout is real: 22% early dropout with ketamine in KetECT. Preparation and an experienced team matter.

### Gaps
- Zhou et al. 2018 and Basso et al. 2020 were not individually retrieved. Their findings are represented only through the 2026 systematic review and the 2022 Dai citation of the Ning group.
- No controlled long-term (>1 year) cognitive data exist for racemic IV, IM, SC or oral ketamine. SUSTAIN-3 is open-label, uncontrolled and industry-funded.
- No quantitative incidence of ketamine cystitis at antidepressant doses was found. SUSTAIN-3 reports no cases, but racemic-ketamine clinic data are lacking.

## 3. Ketamine as ECT anaesthetic or combined with ECT

### Takeaway
Adding low-dose ketamine (0.5 mg/kg) to ECT anaesthesia does not protect memory or speed response in the best RCT (Ketamine-ECT, Lancet Psychiatry 2017). The broader literature is inconsistent. Ketamine-plus-ECT should not be relied on as a cognitive shield.

### Cited Findings
- Ketamine-ECT trial (Anderson et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2017;4:365–377): 79 randomized to ketamine 0.5 mg/kg IV bolus or saline added to the anaesthetic, mostly bilateral brief-pulse ECT. No benefit on the primary outcome, HVLT-R delayed recall after 4 ECTs (difference −0.43, 95% CI −1.73 to 0.87). No benefit on autobiographical memory, verbal fluency, MADRS change or number of ECTs to remission. Remission at end of ECT was 37% (39% ketamine, 35% saline). Any AE occurred in 45% vs 27%. The trial excluded a benefit of effect size ≥0.3 on the primary outcome with 95% confidence — [Lancet Psychiatry](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpsy/article/PIIS2215-0366(17)30077-9/fulltext); [NIHR report](https://www.journalslibrary.nihr.ac.uk/eme/EME04020)
- The NIHR report notes earlier non-randomized studies suggesting ketamine anaesthesia improves reorientation and word recall, but small RCTs were mixed (some showed impaired reorientation). Two meta-analyses of the same 4 RCTs disagreed, and one reported more confusion, disorientation and prolonged delirium with ketamine — [NIHR Journals Library](https://www.journalslibrary.nihr.ac.uk/eme/EME04020); [NCBI Bookshelf](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK425381/)
- Correspondence after the trial argued memory protection may need full anaesthetic ketamine doses of 0.8–1.0 mg/kg — [Lancet Psychiatry correspondence](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpsy/article/PIIS2215-0366(17)30329-2/fulltext) (letter, low evidence)
- Ongoing: San Raffaele phase 3 translational trial of ketamine added to ECT (NCT07088380, n=30, recruiting) — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07088380). An RCT of esketamine vs propofol as ECT anaesthetic in TRD (Zeng et al., J Affect Disord 2024;369) was identified by citation only — [reference list](https://internationalmedicaljournal.org/index.php/ijmhsr/article/view/724)

### Inferences
- If the patient chooses ECT, technique (ultrabrief-pulse high-dose RUL first, with careful switching) matters far more for cognition than adding ketamine. That technique question belongs to the neuromodulation/ECT researcher.

### Gaps
- Results of Zeng 2024 (esketamine vs propofol in ECT) and Kendirlioğlu 2025 (ketamine + ECT combination) were not retrieved.

## 4. Pipeline and under-used options (status October 2026): efficacy numbers, cognition, availability

### Takeaway
- **Most important "near-approval" options:**
  - **Psilocybin (COMP360).** Two positive phase 3s with modest average effects: 3.6–3.8 MADRS points over control. Rolling NDA; final submission Q4 2026; possible US launch H1 2027.

  - **Lithium or T3 augmentation.**
  - **Anti-inflammatory treatment if CRP is high.**
  - **Exercise.**
- **Failures:** esmethadone, zuranolone (for MDD), aticaprant and navacaprant (KOR antagonists), buprenorphine/samidorphan.

**Anti-inflammatory approaches (high CRP)**
- Raison et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2013 (infliximab in TRD): no overall effect. With hs-CRP >5 mg/L, response was 62% (8/13) vs 33% (3/9), NNT about 3.5. With hs-CRP ≤5, placebo did better (57% vs 41%) — [PMC4015348](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4015348)

**Lifestyle and metabolic augmentation**
- Ketogenic diet RCT in TRD (Gao et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2026; n=88, 6 weeks, prepared meals): PHQ-9 drop of −10.5 vs −8.3 on a matched "phyto" control diet. ### Inferences
- **Realistic effect sizes:** psilocybin's phase 3 advantage over placebo (about 3.6–3.8 MADRS points) is smaller than its phase 2b headline. That is roughly an SSRI-augmentation-sized average effect, with a responsive subgroup. LSD's Emerge result (−8.1) and GH001 (−15.5) are much larger but are press-release-only or phase 2b, and functional unblinding inflates psychedelic effect sizes.
- **Eligibility:** GH001's phase 2b excluded current episodes over 2 years. A 10-year chronic course may not qualify for, or respond like, the trial populations. Compass's phase 3 did enroll highly chronic patients (mean episode over 3 years).
- **Psychedelics and cognition:** the burden is confined to the dosing day (minutes for 5-MeO-DMT, about 6 hours for psilocybin and LSD), with no daily drug. That suits a brain-fog-sensitive patient, if the person has no psychosis or bipolar risk.
- **"Failed all classes" claim:** for this patient it should be audited. An adequately dosed MAOI (often underdosed in practice, as in STAR*D), lithium or T3 augmentation, and pramipexole are commonly not tried before ECT. PAX-D's response and remission in a TRD sample (44% and 28%) compare well with esketamine in ESCAPE-TRD (27% remission at week 8). It is cheap, oral and available now off-label. Watch for nausea, somnolence and impulse control.
- **Check CRP:** if hs-CRP is above about 3–5 mg/L, anti-inflammatory augmentation is a reasonable experimental add-on.

### Gaps
- AXS-05 STRIDE-1 TRD results (believed to have missed the primary endpoint in 2020) were not verified in this session.
- No 2025–2026 RCT data were found on buprenorphine monotherapy or low-dose buprenorphine in younger adults with TRD.
- Exact MINDEP-2 effect sizes (HAM-D change by CRP stratum) were not extracted.
- No data were found on response to classic psychedelics in patients who previously failed ketamine or ECT.
- No seltorexant FDA filing date was found. Its cognitive data (an ASCP 2026 poster) were listed but not retrieved.

## 5. Cross-over data: ketamine after ECT failure (and vice versa), and after TMS failure

### Takeaway
Small, mostly uncontrolled data suggest ketamine/esketamine still works in a meaningful minority of ECT non-responders (roughly 20–35% response), with somewhat smaller effects than in ECT-naive patients. I found no systematic data on ECT response after ketamine failure, and no specific data on ketamine after rTMS failure.

### Cited Findings
- Ibrahim et al. 2011: single open-label 0.5 mg/kg infusion in 17 ECT-resistant vs 23 ECT-naive TRD patients. Both groups improved significantly at 230 minutes (effect size d=0.50 in ECT-resistant vs d=1.00 in ECT-naive), and the proportion improving did not differ — [PMC3100439](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3100439/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0278584611001126)
- Retrospective real-world inpatient esketamine study (n=96; 39 with ECT non-response in the current episode, 57 without an adequate ECT course): ECT non-responders had non-significantly lower response and remission. The reported pairs were 34.9% vs 21.9% for response and 24.4% vs 12.1% for remission, with the lower figures belonging to ECT non-responders as the authors state. MADRS still improved significantly in ECT non-responders, and the authors conclude ECT non-response did not significantly affect esketamine outcome — [springermedizin.de](https://www.springermedizin.de/intranasal-esketamine-significantly-alleviates-depression-severi/51165366)
- Spanish expanded-access cohort (n=16, "all options exhausted"): 10/16 responded and 5 remitted on esketamine, with no difference between patients with and without prior ECT — [Vendrell-Serres et al. 2023](https://exa.ai/library/publication/ttrtp7fdh2z)
- KADS: 24% of participants had failed ECT. Adequately dosed SC ketamine still beat midazolam (remission 19.6% vs 2.0%) — [PMC10727911](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10727911/)
- Nitrous oxide proof-of-concept (2015) included 4 prior ECT failures and 3 prior VNS failures among 20 patients — [Zorumski et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-025-03439-6)
- ECT after ECT non-response: in Columbia/OPT-ECT samples, a second course of high-dose bilateral ECT produced high remission rates, at the cost of more persistent retrograde amnesia — [Brain Stimulation 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1935861X20301340) (context only; ECT-technique detail belongs to the neuromodulation researcher)

### Inferences
- For this patient (rTMS failure, no ECT yet), there is no evidence that rTMS failure predicts ketamine failure. Ketamine response predictors that do exist (lower baseline attention or processing speed; outpatient status) are not related to TMS history.
- Because KetECT showed ECT working in patients where ketamine did not reach remission, ketamine non-response should not be taken as a reason to skip ECT.

### Gaps
- No RCT or large cohort reports ECT outcomes specifically in ketamine/esketamine non-responders.
- No study specifically reporting ketamine or esketamine response in rTMS non-responders was found.

## 6. Which options are least likely to cause psychomotor slowing / brain fog?

### Takeaway
The options with the best evidence for **not** adding daily cognitive or psychomotor burden are:
- **Ketamine/esketamine.** Cognition is neutral or improved between doses, with a dosing-day burden only.
- **Intermittent psychedelics**, once approved or in trials. Their effects are confined to the dosing day.
- **T3**, which was better tolerated than lithium in STAR*D.
- **Exercise.**

Pramipexole is effective but commonly causes somnolence or sleep disturbance and nausea. Quetiapine-type augmentation is the most sedating comparator in the head-to-head data. ECT carries the largest short-term memory cost.

### Cited Findings
- **Ketamine:** processing speed improved in 13 of 15 studies, partly independent of mood, with no cognitive deterioration reported — [Chavigny et al. 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/cognitive-effects-of-intravenous-ketamine-in-treatmentresistant-depression-a-systematic-review/171BAC6B88358C98C68E9CE49A34DA01). Processing-speed gains lasted 5 weeks after infusions — [Zavaliangos-Petropulu 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37060953/). Cognition was better than with ECT at 3 weeks — [Kumpf et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40900112/)
- **Esketamine:** stable cognition over up to about 6.5 years, with small reaction-time slowing mainly in those 65 and older. Somnolence was reported as an AE in 23.1% but almost all dosing-day AEs resolved the same day — [Zaki et al. 2025](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/a3adba8e-3d34-47a5-a37b-6a7137ab0c05); [NPP 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41386-023-01577-5)
- **Quetiapine XR comparator:** discontinuations for sedation (2.1%), weight gain (1.8%) and somnolence (1.5%) exceeded esketamine's in ESCAPE-TRD — [European Psychiatry](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-psychiatry/article/esketamine-nasal-spray-shows-higher-remission-and-response-rates-over-32-weeks-of-treatment-compared-with-quetiapine-extendedrelease-in-patients-with-treatment-resistant-depression-results-from-escapetrd-a-randomised-phase-iiib-clinical-trial/F7BAD645749E43865CB8815D8BED9559)
- **ECT:** HVLT-R delayed recall dropped about 9.7 T-points by end of course, recovering by 1 month. Self-rated memory stayed lower through 6 months. Persisting amnesia occurred in a minority in KetECT — [NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2302399); [Ekstrand 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35020871)
- **Pramipexole:** nausea, headache, dizziness, and sleep disturbance or somnolence were the main AEs. 20% stopped for intolerance — [PAX-D](https://acplab.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/BrowningGeddes25.pdf)
- **T3 vs lithium:** T3 had "fewer troublesome side effects" than lithium in STAR*D — [NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/funding/clinical-research/practical/stard/allmedicationlevels)
- **Psychedelics:** GH001 median psychoactive duration was 11 minutes, with discharge readiness at 1 hour in 99% of visits — [IJNP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article-pdf/29/Supplement_1/i81/71009728/pyag040.150.pdf). DT120 dosing-day effects resolved in a mean of about 5.8 hours, with 99% of AEs mild to moderate and transient — [Definium](https://ir.definiumtx.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/232/definium-therapeutics-announces-positive-topline-results-from-phase-3-emerge-study-of-dt120-orally-disintegrating-tablet-odt-in-major-depressive-disorder). COMP360 TEAEs were mostly transient on the dosing day — [SEC 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1816590/000181659026000047/cmps-20260707.htm)
- **Ketogenic diet:** no difference vs control diet in cognitive-impairment secondary outcomes — [Gao et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12874075/)
- **Nitrous oxide:** AEs (nausea, light-headedness) are transient and much lower at 25% than 50% — [Nagele 2021](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scitranslmed.abe1376); [Myles 2025](https://www.bpsgos.org/article/S2667-1743(25)00058-8/fulltext)
- **Zuranolone:** the FDA cited safety concerns about adverse effects when declining the MDD indication — [Psychiatric Times](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/next-steps-for-zuranolone-as-a-treatment-for-mdd)
- **Seltorexant:** J&J describes it as improving sleep "without next-day sedation" (company claim). A cognition poster exists, but its results were not retrieved — [J&J press release](https://www.jnj.com/media-center/press-releases/johnson-johnson-elevates-leadership-in-depression-with-new-data-at-2026-american-college-of-neuropsychopharmacology-annual-meeting)

### Inferences
- **For a patient whose main complaint is drug-induced psychomotor slowing:** intermittent, clinic-administered treatments avoid daily exposure and allow scheduling around study or work. These are esketamine or ketamine, and later psilocybin, LSD or 5-MeO-DMT.
- **Daily oral options least likely to sedate**, in my reading of mechanism plus trial AE profiles, not head-to-head cognitive trials:
  - T3 augmentation.
  - Tranylcypromine, which is pharmacologically activating; insomnia, rather than sedation, is the typical MAOI complaint (inferred, not directly sourced here).
  - Pramipexole, if titrated slowly; somnolence is still possible.
- **Sedating classes are the worst fit:** quetiapine and other sedating antipsychotics, mirtazapine, GABA-ergic neurosteroids such as zuranolone, and possibly lithium at higher levels.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head cognitive trials compare tranylcypromine, pramipexole, lithium or T3 in TRD. Statements about their relative "brain fog" are inferred from AE profiles.
- No cognitive outcome data from the psilocybin or LSD phase 3 programs were found.
- The patient's specific failed drugs and doses are unknown, so whether MAOIs, lithium/T3 and pramipexole were adequately tried cannot be judged from the available information.
