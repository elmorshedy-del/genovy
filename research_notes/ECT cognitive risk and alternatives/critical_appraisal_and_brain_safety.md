# Critical Appraisal of the ECT Cognitive-Side-Effect Literature and the Evidence on Structural Brain Damage

Scope note for the report writer: these notes "research the research". They cover how reliable the commonly quoted cognitive-risk figures for ECT are, the biases that push those figures in each direction, the main critiques and rebuttals, practice variation between centres, the neurobiological evidence (imaging, biomarkers, neuropathology, animal work), dementia-risk cohorts, and a GRADE-style verdict. Patient context: young adult, about 10 years of treatment-resistant depression (TRD), very prone to side effects (psychomotor slowing, brain fog), planning to attend medical school. Search date: October 2026.

---

## 1. Measurement problems: are the standard tools able to detect the harm patients describe?

### Takeaway
The reassuring headline ("deficits are limited to about 3 days to 2 weeks, and cognition then returns to or exceeds baseline") rests mostly on anterograde and executive tests and screening instruments (MMSE, MoCA). These tools have ceiling effects, practice effects, and a baseline lowered by depression. They also exclude retrograde autobiographical memory, which is the domain patients complain about most. The only purpose-built retrograde tool, the Columbia AMI and its short form (CUAMI / AMI-SF), uses a consistency score that lacks published normative data. Its validity was disputed for a decade (Semkovska and McLoughlin versus Sackeim). Even so, it is the instrument that most consistently detects persistent deficits and that tracks patients' own reports.

### Cited Findings
- **Semkovska & McLoughlin 2010 meta-analysis** (Biol Psychiatry 68(6):568–577, doi:10.1016/j.biopsych.2010.06.009). It pooled 24 cognitive variables from 84 studies (2,981 patients). Performance dropped 0–3 days post-ECT in 72% of variables, with effect sizes from −1.10 (95% CI −1.53 to −0.67) to −0.21. From 4–15 days, all but one CI included zero or was positive. After 15 days there were no negative effect sizes, and 57% of variables showed improvement beyond baseline (ES 0.35 to 0.75). The authors explicitly state "No standardized retrograde amnesia tests were identified." — [Biological Psychiatry abstract](https://www.biologicalpsychiatryjournal.com/article/S0006-3223(10)00598-6/abstract); [NCBI/DARE summary](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK80031/?report=printable)
- The independent DARE appraisal of that meta-analysis found that "case reports and studies that used self-reported measures were excluded". It also found that the authors "did not state that they assessed validity" and advised "some caution… due to a failure to assess study validity". The analysis used a fixed-effect model. — [NCBI/DARE summary](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK80031/?report=printable); [Academia.edu copy of DARE summary](https://www.academia.edu/94003015/Objective_Cognitive_Performance_Associated_with_Electroconvulsive_Therapy_for_Depression_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta_Analysis)
- Kirov et al. (2016), themselves ECT proponents, concede that the 2010 meta-analysis "does not apply to retrograde amnesia, which was not part of this analysis, and it cannot be extended to cognitive functions that were not tested." — [Kirov et al. 2016, BJPsych 208:266–270, doi:10.1192/bjp.bp.114.158261](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/103696/)
- **MMSE insensitivity.** A 2000–2017 meta-analysis covered 91 studies (n=3,762), which I believe is Landry et al., J ECT 2021 (authorship not confirmed in this search; PMID 33009218). It found "no significant changes in global cognition with Mini-Mental State Examination" immediately post-ECT. By contrast, autobiographical memory, verbal fluency and verbal memory fell at 1–28 days (small-to-medium Hedges g). Verbal-fluency deficits were greater in younger adults. The authors conclude that the MMSE's "clinical utility is extremely limited to track post-ECT cognitive changes" and that assessment "should include autobiographical memory, verbal fluency, and verbal memory." — [PubMed 33009218](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009218/)
- **Ceiling effects and a baseline lowered by depression, seen in UK audit data.** In the ECTAS 2021 national dataset, MMSE rose from 24.9 to 26.7 (n=596) and MoCA from 23.3 to 24.8 (n=306) after ECT. Stratification shows the gain is driven by patients who were cognitively impaired at baseline. Those with MMSE 1–22 rose from 16.4 to 22.4, while those starting at 23–30 actually *fell* slightly, from 27.0 to 26.0 (p=0.005). The longer ACE-III showed a 1.3-point drop (n.s., n=19). One clinic using the Hodges scale found a significant 0.7-point deterioration, "the only scale showing deterioration that reaches statistical significance." Scores of 0 (often meaning "too unwell to test") were excluded. — [ECTAS 2021 dataset report](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/improving-care/ccqi/quality-networks/electro-convulsive-therapy-clinics-(ectas)/dataset-reports/ectas-2021-dataset-report.pdf?sfvrsn=59757c5c_5)
- ECTAS 2022 showed the same pattern: MMSE 25.5→26.6 (n=433), MoCA 22.8→24.4 (n=322). Patients scoring above 22 at baseline "showed virually no change on either scale", while those under 23 improved by about 5 points. ECTAS also notes that referring clinicians often run these tests and "many of these clinicians will have had little, if any, formal training in their use." — [ECTAS 2022 dataset report](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/improving-care/ccqi/quality-networks/electro-convulsive-therapy-clinics-(ectas)/ectas-dataset-2022-report.pdf)
- **Semkovska & McLoughlin's critique of the AMI.** They argued that normal autobiographical recall loses consistency over time, that depression itself reduces recall of specific memories, and that few attempts had been made "to accurately measure the specific effect of ECT on AM independent of both normal and mood-associated forgetting", which "prevents us at present from objectively quantifying the nature and extent of RAA associated with ECT." — [Semkovska & McLoughlin 2013, J ECT 29:127–133 (record)](https://exa.ai/library/publication/1x168120f6q)
- **Semkovska et al. 2012 normative/validation study** (Psychiatry Research). The CAMI-SF was given twice, 6 months apart, to 44 healthy volunteers, who "showed significant large decreases in retrieval consistency on all components". Over 2 months, 30 severely depressed patients never treated with ECT and 19 healthy controls showed "equivalent amounts of consistency loss". At reassessment, "only patients with persisting depressive symptoms were distinguishable from controls on episodic-specific memories". The authors note the CAMI-SF had been used "exclusively" in ECT patients "without previous published report of normative data". — [Semkovska, Noone, Carton, McLoughlin 2012 (SDU portal)](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/measuring-consistency-of-autobiographical-memory-recall-in-depres/)
- **Sackeim's rebuttal (2014)** called the critique "factually incorrect". He said normative data from repeatedly tested matched healthy controls "had, in fact, been collected… in all but one study". In the community "Services" study, AMI-SF scores were adjusted for the change seen in the comparison sample, and the deficit was still "profound both immediately and 6 months after ECT". He added that the CUAMI/CUAMI-SF are "the only neuropsychological tests repeatedly shown to covary with patient self-evaluations of ECT's effects on memory". He conceded "there are no formal reports on inter-rater reliability… or test-retest reliability of the key dependent measures", and that methods sections in most of his group's reports "did not mention the existence of a matched normal control group". — [Sackeim 2014, J ECT 30:177–186 (PMC4141894)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4141894/); [PubMed 24755727](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24755727/)
- In unpublished data Sackeim presented in 2014, every ECT group differed from healthy controls immediately post-ECT except ultrabrief-pulse right unilateral (RUL), which did not differ. — [PubMed 24755727 figure legends](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24755727/)
- In a follow-up, "Final thoughts on the bathwater", Sackeim said that patients given bilateral (BL) ECT, wider pulse widths and more treatments "had poorer amnesia scores two and six months following treatment". He added that Semkovska & McLoughlin "do not dispute" that deficits on these instruments are larger than on any other neuropsychological measure. — [Sackeim, J ECT (PMC7727876)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7727876/)
- **An independent Dutch evaluation of the CUAMI-SF** (60 participants, only 14 ECT patients) states: "no study has ever been published with the explicit aim of determining the validity and reliability of the CUAMI-SF, and normative data are also lacking". It adds that the Kopelman AMI, developed for dementia, may lack sensitivity to post-ECT loss. Consistency scores were "much lower in patients after ECT than after DBS as well as in a healthy population." — [UvA repository, Translation and Critical Evaluation of the CUAMI-SF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/271034205/Translation_and_Critical_Evaluation_of_the_Columbia_University.pdf)
- **Mathiassen et al. 2025** (BJPsych; senior authors include Semkovska, previously the main AMI critic) included only longitudinal studies with control groups (9 studies; 432 ECT patients, 173 controls). Post-ECT autobiographical memory loss exceeded controls' (SMD 0.55; 95% CI 0.35–0.75; I²=35%). By placement: RUL SMD 0.32 (0.06–0.57), BL 0.82 (0.49–1.15), mixed 0.64. Number needed to harm for BL versus RUL was 5. Memory "was stable at long-term follow-up" (6–12 months), "indicating that lost memories are not regained". Only 4 studies had long-term follow-up, so no long-term meta-analysis was possible. Egger's test showed publication bias (t=2.22, p=0.022), although fail-safe N was 180. All included studies used brief-pulse ECT. — [Mathiassen et al. 2025, BJPsych, doi:10.1192/bjp.2025.2 (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/autobiographical-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/8F694868E2767C11F75DBA2C341C21AF); [PubMed 40357797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40357797/)
- A poster version of the same review translated the effect: AM consistency fell 13.3% (SD 17.4) more than in healthy controls and 9.9% (SD 8.0) more than in depressed controls. — [Region Hovedstaden poster 2023](https://pure.regionh.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/107727173/Poster_pr_sentation_AM_review_and_meta_analysis_ved_RHP_forskningsdag_2023_v2.pdf)
- **Stachura et al. 2025 mixed-methods review** (conference abstract; 22 quantitative and 20 qualitative studies). ECT caused greater retrograde autobiographical amnesia (RAA) than other treatments (SMD −0.73; 95% CI −1.31 to −0.15), and BL caused more than RUL (SMD −0.29). Pre–post effects were large with very high heterogeneity (I²=93%). "The main effect moderator was RAA assessment tool," and "RAA measurement is not unified." — [Stachura et al. 2025 abstract](https://exa.ai/library/publication/vrzzcb94ys4)
- Porter et al. (2020, BJPsych Open), a UK consensus on monitoring: "the finding of a mean score that is not reduced from baseline cannot be taken to indicate that impairment, particularly of new learning, cannot occur in individuals." They also note that "the evidence for schedules of testing to monitor cognitive side-effects is currently limited." — [Porter et al. 2020, PMC7191622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622)
- Kirov's group agreed in correspondence that "future studies should include more sensitive tests, as there could be subtle cognitive functions that are affected by ECT, which our tests didn't pick up." They also acknowledged the problem of a baseline measured while depressed: "We can't see an easy way out of this problem." — [Kirov & Atkins, Authors' reply, BJPsych 2016](https://exa.ai/library/publication/nydyj523xtz)

### Inferences
- The well-known "returns to baseline within 2 weeks" finding is likely accurate for group-mean anterograde and executive performance on standard tests. It says almost nothing about retrograde autobiographical memory, which those meta-analyses never measured. Because the pre-ECT baseline is lowered by depression, "return to baseline" may still mean "below the patient's premorbid level". Sackeim (2007) made the same point.
- Screening tools (MMSE, MoCA, 6CIT) are poorly suited to a high-functioning young adult. Ceiling effects mean small but meaningful losses, for example in the verbal learning, processing speed and memory consolidation needed in medical school, would go undetected. A fair individual baseline needs a comprehensive battery: verbal learning such as RAVLT/HVLT, verbal fluency, processing speed, and an autobiographical memory measure (CUAMI-SF or similar) given before ECT and repeated at 2 and 6 months.
- The AMI dispute has largely converged. Once Semkovska co-authored a 2025 meta-analysis concluding that ECT causes autobiographical memory loss that is not regained by 6–12 months, the strongest methodological critic and the strongest defender of the instrument agreed on direction. They still disagree on absolute magnitude, and normative data remain lacking.

### Gaps
- No published normative or test-retest data for CUAMI-SF consistency scores in general-population samples of different ages were found. It is unclear what a "normal" 6-month loss of consistency is for a 20–30-year-old.
- No validated instrument measures the specific memories patients report losing, such as coursework, skills, or events from the months or years before treatment, as opposed to a fixed list of events.
- The Landry et al. attribution for PMID 33009218 is from memory and was not confirmed in search results.

## 2. Biases that could UNDERSTATE harm, and the critiques and rebuttals

### Takeaway
Real understating biases exist: large follow-up attrition, mostly short follow-up, group means hiding a vulnerable subgroup, exclusion of self-report and retrograde measures from the flagship meta-analysis, insensitive screening tools, and publication bias even in the AMI literature. Sackeim's own 2007 community study found persistent retrograde amnesia at 6 months, with 12.4% meeting criteria for "marked and persistent" amnesia, and he wrote that it is "inaccurate and inadvisable to continue to deny that ECT can exert long-term adverse effects". Critics led by John Read go further and argue that harm is common and efficacy unproven. Their reviews have been formally criticised as methodologically weak (AMSTAR-2 "critically low"), and Read has a declared conflict as a paid expert witness. In 2025 a US federal jury found that one ECT device maker had failed to warn, but not that this failure caused the plaintiff's injury.

### Cited Findings
**Sackeim et al. 2007, the largest prospective community study, and its admissions**
- 751 patients were referred to 7 New York-area hospitals and 347 took part in at least one post-ECT assessment. The degrees of freedom at 6 months (for example F(6,240) for the AMI-SF) imply roughly 240–250 patients were assessed then. — [Sackeim et al. 2007, Neuropsychopharmacology 32:244–254, doi:10.1038/sj.npp.1301180](https://www.nature.com/articles/1301180.pdf); [full text PDF](http://www.ectresources.org/ECTscience/Sackeim_et_al__2007___AAA___Long-term_memory_and_cognitive_dysfunction_____Bilateral_worse_.pdf)
- "Adverse cognitive effects were detected 6 months following the acute treatment course." Sine-wave stimulation caused slowing of reaction time that persisted at 6 months. BL ECT caused "more severe and persisting retrograde amnesia than right unilateral ECT", and 6-month amnesia rose linearly with the number of BL treatments but was unrelated to the number of RUL or bifrontal treatments. Of 306 patients classified, 38 (12.4%) met a priori criteria for marked and persistent retrograde amnesia, and only receipt of BL ECT distinguished this group. For BL-only patients, AMI-SF decrements were 3.4 times (post-ECT) and 2.8 times (6 months) the forgetting seen in healthy comparison groups. Risk factors were older age, lower premorbid IQ and female sex. — [Sackeim et al. 2007 full text](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf)
- The same paper adds a caveat that cuts the other way: "the findings do not indicate that the treatments with more benign outcomes are free of long-term effects", and "without evaluating a comparable group that did not receive ECT, it cannot be concluded… that the extent of improvement in any group returned to premorbid levels." — [Sackeim et al. 2007 full text](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf)
- Sackeim 2014: "It is inaccurate and inadvisable to continue to deny that ECT can exert long-term adverse effects in this domain." — [Sackeim 2014, PMC4141894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4141894/)

**Attrition and short follow-up**
- In the Swedish PREFECT study (8 hospitals), 535 consented but only 277 were included in the final long-term analysis, with median follow-up of just 73 days. — [Sigström et al. 2020, BJPsych Open (PMC7176828)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7176828)
- In Mathiassen 2025, only 4 of 9 controlled studies had any follow-up of 6 months or more. Funnel-plot asymmetry suggested publication bias toward *larger* ECT–control differences. — [Mathiassen et al. 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/autobiographical-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/8F694868E2767C11F75DBA2C341C21AF)
- The 2010 objective-cognition meta-analysis classed anything beyond 15 days as "long term" and excluded self-report measures and case reports. — [NCBI/DARE summary](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK80031/?report=printable)

**Group means masking a subgroup; mismatch between patients' and clinicians' accounts**
- Porter et al. 2020 say a non-reduced mean "cannot be taken to indicate that impairment… cannot occur in individuals". Their recommended patient information states that retrograde loss "is normally worse for the time immediately before ECT (3 months) – but sometimes extends back for up to 1 year and may occasionally extend beyond this… some of the memories… will not return." — [Porter et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7191622)
- The method of asking matters. In PREFECT, rating subjective memory before and after with the CPRS item showed worsening in 16.2%, no change in 52.3% and improvement in 31.4%. Yet when asked directly afterwards to evaluate ECT's effect on memory, 54.6% reported a negative effect. 7.9% had clinically significant post-ECT impairment together with worsening. — [Sigström et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7176828)
- Rose et al. (2003, BMJ systematic review), as summarised by Read: four studies (n=703) that asked patients about "persistent or permanent memory loss" gave rates of 29%–55%, average 38%. Meechan et al. note that review "has been criticised (Bergsholm, 2012)" because some included studies collected self-report much sooner after treatment. — [Read et al. 2025, J Med Ethics (PMC12911569)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12911569); [Meechan et al. 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/9661DA40F061600ED202C716C154E4BB/S2045796021000731a.pdf/div-class-title-a-critique-of-narrative-reviews-of-the-evidence-base-for-ect-in-depression-div.pdf)
- A 2015 ECTAS patient survey reportedly found 19% of patients affected by permanent memory loss. This is reported second-hand by campaigner Sue Cunliffe via Psychiatric Times; the primary ECTAS document was not retrieved. — [Psychiatric Times, "ECT: Dangerous on Either Side of the Pond"](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/ect-dangerous-either-side-pond)

**Critic reviews: Read & Bentall 2010; Read, Kirsch & McGrath 2019/2020**
- Read & Bentall (2010, Epidemiologia e Psichiatria Sociale) concluded, as quoted by critics: "Given the strong evidence… of persistent and, for some, permanent brain dysfunction, primarily evidenced in the form of retrograde and anterograde amnesia, and… a slight but significant increased risk of death, the cost-benefit analysis for ECT is so poor that its use cannot be scientifically justified." — [quoted in Meechan et al. 2022, PMC8851059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8851059/)
- Read, Kirsch & McGrath (2019, Ethical Human Psychology and Psychiatry 21(2):64–103, doi:10.1891/EHPP-D-19-00014) reviewed the 11 ECT-versus-sham RCTs (all pre-1986) and the 5 meta-analyses built on them. Mean quality score was 12.3/24. "None convincingly demonstrate that they are double-blind," and average size was 37 people. At end of treatment, 4 trials favoured ECT, 5 showed no difference and 2 were mixed. Only two higher-quality studies reported follow-up, with effect sizes of 0.065 (favouring ECT) and 0.299 (favouring sham). The authors called for immediate suspension of ECT "given the high risk of permanent memory loss and the small mortality risk." — [Read, Kirsch & McGrath abstract (BJPsych Advances listing)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-advances/article/electroconvulsive-therapy-for-depression-a-review-of-the-quality-of-ect-versus-sham-ect-trials-and-metaanalyses/8B8A6FBE2A609D43DFC77ED778F8F935)

**Rebuttals: Meechan et al. 2022, Anderson 2021, Andrade 2021**
- Meechan, Laws, Young, McLoughlin & Jauhar (2022, Epidemiol Psychiatr Sci 31:e10, doi:10.1017/S2045796021000731) rated both Read reviews "critically low" on AMSTAR-2, with 100% inter-rater agreement (κ=1). Their criticisms: no pre-registered protocol; an unvalidated 24-point quality scale; collapsing Cochrane's "unclear" into "no", which they say inflates negative ratings; and "vote counting". Their own re-meta-analysis of the trials in Read & Bentall found a large pooled effect favouring ECT (g=−0.85, 95% CI −1.08 to −0.63). They argue Read and colleagues relied on one patient-perspective review (Rose 2003) while omitting six subjective-memory studies with contrary results, and included only 1 of 75 objective memory studies. — [Meechan et al. 2022, PMC8851059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8851059/); [Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/9661DA40F061600ED202C716C154E4BB/S2045796021000731a.pdf/div-class-title-a-critique-of-narrative-reviews-of-the-evidence-base-for-ect-in-depression-div.pdf)
- Anderson (2021, BJPsych Advances) rejected the claim that prior-ECT patients were unblinded "because ECT is always followed by headaches and temporary confusion", citing SEAN audit rates of about 30% headache and 20% confusion. He judged Read et al. "a polemic dressed in the clothes of a scientific review" that ignored dose–response and placement RCTs. — [Anderson 2021, BJPsych Advances](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-advances/article/electroconvulsive-therapy-ect-versus-sham-ect-for-depression-do-study-limitations-invalidate-the-evidence-and-mean-we-should-stop-using-ect/392825F99367AEA47EAB4A62DDF25087)
- Andrade (2021, J Clin Psychiatry) called Read's harm claims "hyperbolic": "No acknowledgment is made that the cognitive deficits are mostly subtle, detected only on formal testing, and have no impact on functioning in everyday life." He also corrected Read's reading of a suicide study (odds ratio 1.31, not significant) and cited Liang et al., where ECT reduced the suicide hazard (HR 0.79 unipolar; 0.81 bipolar). — [Andrade 2021, "If ECT Is a Placebo…", JCP](https://www.psychiatrist.com/jcp/if-ect-is-placebo-we-need-more-placebos-in-psychiatry-reply-to-read/)
- Read's replies: (a) in JCP 2021, a review of non-placebo trials found none "produced robust evidence that ECT is effective for depression… at least 60% maintained ECT participants on medication and 89% produced no meaningful follow-up data beyond the end of treatment" ([Read 2021, JCP letter](https://www.psychiatrist.com/jcp/response-to-dr-andrades-commentary-on-our-review-ect-efficacy-research/)); (b) in 2022 he argued that Meechan et al. used AMSTAR-2, a tool for systematic reviews, while calling his work "narrative". He noted that the most recent meta-analysis, Mutz et al. 2019 (senior author Young, a Meechan co-author), used only 1 of the 11 sham RCTs for efficacy, excluding two Lancet trials as "Cannot be obtained". He conceded "we should have conducted the search formally… and reported the process" ([Read 2022, Epidemiol Psychiatr Sci (PMC8967695)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8967695/))
- Note that this exchange is mainly about **efficacy versus sham**. The memory-harm claims in Read's reviews depend heavily on patient-report surveys, not controlled objective data. — [Meechan et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8851059/)

**Read's 2025 surveys (convenience samples) and conflict of interest**
- Survey of 858 ECT recipients and 286 relatives or friends from 44 countries (January–September 2024). Only 17.2% of recipients recalled being told ECT "can cause long-term or permanent memory problems", against 63.4% for "temporary memory problems". The paper declares that "JR has been a paid expert witness in several ECT legal cases." — [Read et al. 2025/2026, J Med Ethics (PMC12911569)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12911569); [BMJ Group press release](https://bmjgroup.com/medical-pros-of-electroconvulsive-therapy-ect-exaggerated-while-cons-downplayed-survey-findings-suggest)
- Companion 2025 papers from the same survey report that most respondents described memory loss lasting more than 3 years. Seventeen of 25 listed adverse effects were reported by more than half: losing train of thought 88%, difficulty concentrating 86%, fatigue 80%, emotional blunting 76%. — [MedicalXpress on Read et al. 2025, Int J Ment Health, doi:10.1080/00207411.2025.2576946](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2025-11-electroconvulsive-therapy-linked-wide-range.html); [EurekAlert release listing the 2025 EHPP papers](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1124501)

**Professional bodies**
- APA Task Force Report, 2025 edition, as quoted by Read: "major risks" include "adverse effects on… central nervous systems (including both transient and persistent cognitive adverse effects)". It also states that "some individuals may report having memory problems that remain for months or years, or even permanently" and that "bilateral ECT is more likely than right unilateral to lead to memory difficulty". — [quoted in Read et al. 2025, PMC12911569](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12911569)
- RANZCP: anterograde changes "generally return to normal or may be improved… within 2-4 weeks", while "it is also possible that autobiographical memory impairment may persist permanently, the risk of this varying with the type of ECT treatment approach." — [RANZCP ECT position statement](https://www.ranzcp.org/clinical-guidelines-publications/clinical-guidelines-publications-library/electroconvulsive-therapy-ect)
- An NHS (Oxford Health) patient page cites "7% of people receiving unilateral ECT report some persistent memory loss 12 months after ECT" and notes that 17% say their memory was already a problem before ECT. — [Oxford Health NHS, side effects of ECT](https://oxfordhealth.nhs.uk/ips/ect/what-are-the-side-effects-of-ect)
- RCPsych, in response to Read's 2020 audit and call for suspension, said ECT should not be suspended for "some forms of severe mental illness". — [BBC News 2020](https://www.bbc.com/news/uk-52900074)
- In 2023, several Northern Ireland health trusts reviewed ECT patient leaflets after research suggested patients were being misled about risks. An RCPsych spokesperson said its own resource "meets health information standards". — [BBC News NI 2023](https://www.bbc.com/news/uk-northern-ireland-66587095)
- NICE 2025 surveillance of TA59/NG222 excluded studies with fewer than 60 participants. It concluded that new evidence "supports and aligns with current recommendations" for depression, while "concerns about cognitive side effects, such as memory impairment, highlight the importance of careful patient information and risk-benefit assessment". TA59 recommendations are to be folded into condition-specific guidelines. — [NICE 2025 surveillance consultation document](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta59/documents/consultation-document); [NICE surveillance proposal](https://www.nice.org.uk/consultations/3133/2)

**Litigation**
- UK: Freeths LLP has been preparing claims for patients alleging long-term memory loss, centred on failure to warn and informed consent rather than proof of brain damage. The Mail on Sunday reported in July 2020 that "dozens" were suing, and an advocacy site says "over 100". No court outcome was found. — [CEPUK blog 2022](http://cepuk.org/2022/01/17/blog-new-nice-guidelines-for-ect-are-dangerously-inadequate-say-50-patients-and-professionals); [Psychiatric Times](https://www.psychiatrictimes.com/view/ect-dangerous-either-side-pond); [Life After ECT (advocacy site)](https://lifeafterect.org/a-call-for-an-independent-review-into-the-practice-of-ect-electroconvulsive-therapy); [Read 2023 letter/report noting Freeths cases](https://www.dbrecoveryresources.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/ECT-Therapy-Prof-John-Read-August-2023.pdf)
- US, *Thelen v. Somatics*: the plaintiff received 95 ECT treatments between 2014 and 2016 and was diagnosed with a neurocognitive disorder in 2017. In 2023 the jury found Somatics failed to adequately warn but that the failure "was not the proximate cause of any injuries". The 11th Circuit affirmed on September 29, 2025. The consent form he signed had already warned of "permanent memory loss" but not "brain damage". — [Thelen v. Somatics, 11th Cir. No. 23-13892 (FindLaw)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-11th-circuit/117778022.html); [Wisner Baum (plaintiff firm) blog](https://www.wisnerbaum.com/blog/2023/august/electroshock-therapy-ect-trial-jury-finds-somati)
- Somatics added a "permanent brain damage" risk statement in an October 2018 regulatory update to the Thymatron manual. Its current manual says this warning exists "in the interests of avoiding litigation" because Scientology and CCHR "have threatened to sue… regardless of any evidence about it" (dated 7/3/24). — [Thymatron System IV manual Rev22](https://thymatron.com/downloads/System_IV_Instruction_Manual_Rev22.pdf); [Mad in America 2018](https://www.madinamerica.com/2018/11/ect-manufacturer-warns-permanent-brain-damage)

### Inferences
- The strongest evidence for persistent harm comes from within the ECT research establishment (Sackeim 2007, Sackeim 2014, Mathiassen/Semkovska/Videbech 2025), not from critics. Persistent autobiographical memory loss is therefore not a fringe claim. Disagreement is about size, prevalence and how much depression contributes, not about whether it exists.
- Read's efficacy reviews have real methodological weaknesses, and his patient surveys are self-selected convenience samples. Respondents who feel harmed are likely to be over-represented, and he has a financial conflict as an expert witness. They are useful evidence that severe, lasting harm occurs and that consent is often incomplete. They cannot estimate how common harm is.
- Proponent claims that cognitive deficits are "mostly subtle… no impact on functioning" (Andrade) go beyond the evidence. The tools used are insensitive (Section 1), follow-up is short, and there are almost no functional outcomes such as academic or occupational performance.
- Both the US manufacturer warning and the jury's failure-to-warn finding are shaped by litigation. They are not scientific evidence of brain damage. The jury did not find causation, and the manufacturer says its warning was added defensively.

### Gaps
- No study was found that reports cognitive outcomes separately for patients lost to follow-up, so the direction of attrition bias is untested. It could mean the harmed disengage, or that the well stop attending.
- No primary-source data on industry funding or authorship in the ECT cognitive literature was retrieved. Device-manufacturer ties of key authors could not be verified in this search.
- The primary ECTAS 2015 patient survey (19% "permanent memory loss") was not retrieved.
- No current UK court judgment on ECT memory-loss or consent claims was found.
- The text of the original Andrade 2021 JCP commentary and of Read et al. 2021 (rebuttal to Kirov et al.) was not retrieved; only the follow-up letters were.

## 3. Biases that could OVERSTATE harm

### Takeaway
Several confounds plausibly inflate harm estimates. Depression itself impairs cognition and autobiographical recall, and residual depression strongly predicts subjective memory complaints. Patient expectations shape reports. Older sine-wave and high-dose bilateral techniques inflate historical figures. ECT patients are the most severely ill. Concurrent medication and anaesthesia add noise. Better studies partly correct for these, and a residual ECT-specific autobiographical effect survives depressed and healthy control comparisons. The confounds explain much, but not all, of the reported harm.

### Cited Findings
- **Depression and the AMI baseline.** Depressed patients never treated with ECT produced fewer episodic-specific memories than controls and lost consistency at the same rate. Only those with persisting depressive symptoms differed from controls at re-test. — [Semkovska et al. 2012](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/measuring-consistency-of-autobiographical-memory-recall-in-depres/)
- **Residual depression drives subjective complaints.** In a Swedish register study (n=1,498, with 6-month data from the Swedish National Quality Register for ECT, Q-ECT), 25.2% had subjective memory worsening at 6 months. Mean MADRS-S was 22.0 among those with worsening versus 13.8 without. Higher MADRS-S, younger age and female sex were associated with worse outcomes (OR 1.06 per MADRS-S point; 1.47 female; 2.69 for the younger age contrast in the follow-up model). 62.2% of those with worsening had reported no memory impairment before ECT. — [Tornhamre et al. 2025, J Affect Disord (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S016503272402007X); [PubMed 39644929](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39644929)
- Tornhamre et al. cite Fernie et al. 2014 and others as linking subjective memory worsening to depressive symptoms and to non-response. — [Tornhamre et al. 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S016503272402007X)
- **Expectations.** In PREFECT, subjective worsening was predicted by more negative pre-ECT expectations about memory (OR 1.83–1.90 per point), younger age, and shorter follow-up. — [Sigström et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7176828); [Cambridge PDF with ORs](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B11433F4BE1F19DBA4C60B887DA63446/S2056472420000095a.pdf/long-term-subjective-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy.pdf)
- **Short-term subjective worsening and technique.** In a Swedish register study of 1,212 patients (I believe Brus et al., J ECT 2017; authorship not confirmed in search results), 26% reported subjective memory worsening within 1 week: 31% of women versus 18% of men, and 32% at age 18–39 versus 22% at 65+. Remission lowered the risk. Brief pulse carried higher risk than ultrabrief (OR 1.61; 95% CI 1.05–2.47). — [science.gov summary](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/u/unilateral+rul+ect); [full text PDF](http://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/yct50139-96.103.pdf)
- **Outdated technique inflates historical figures.** Sine-wave stimulation produced persistent reaction-time slowing, and BL produced the most persistent retrograde amnesia. The APA had already said in 2001 there is "no justification" for sine wave. — [Sackeim et al. 2007](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf)
- Ultrabrief RUL ECT did not differ from healthy controls on CUAMI consistency immediately post-ECT in Sackeim's unpublished trial data. — [Sackeim 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24755727/)
- **Selection of the most severely ill.** In the ECTAS 2021 data, 57% were detained under the Mental Health Act at the start of treatment (up from 42% in 2012/13), and 96% were rated moderately to severely ill on CGI. — [Review of ECTAS data 2012–2021, BJPsych Open 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/review-of-published-ectas-data-set-from-20122013-to-2021/3DF453AE0AB3836CD74B6C8C5C5C4568)
- Kirov et al. found that age and depression severity at testing, not cumulative ECT number, predicted cognitive scores (199 patients, 498 assessments, 0–186 sessions). — [Kirov et al. 2016, PubMed 26585101](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26585101/)
- **Concurrent medication.** A recent review concluded that "current evidence does not show a significant increase in cognitive side effects associated with lithium use during ECT", while noting methodological limitations. — [Lithium, Electroconvulsive Therapy and Cognition, PubMed 41949408](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41949408); [ResearchGate version](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/370639738_Delirium_with_Concurrent_Use_of_Lithium_and_ECT_and_the_Safety_Implications_Case_Reports_and_Review_of_the_Literature)
- Evidence on anaesthetic agent and cognition conflicts. One meta-analysis, summarised by an insurer policy bulletin, suggested ketamine anaesthesia brought greater cognitive side effects than propofol. Another study (Zou et al., cited in a 2025 review) found less post-ECT cognitive impairment with ketofol than with propofol. — [Aetna Clinical Policy Bulletin 0445 (secondary)](https://www.aetna.com/cpb/medical/data/400_499/0445.html); [Sukhdeo et al. 2025, Am J Geriatr Psychiatry](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1064748125004336)
- **Counterweight (residual ECT effect after controls).** Against depressed (non-ECT) controls, the AMI effect was SMD 0.76 (0.39–1.11, k=6). Against healthy controls it was 0.44 (0.14–0.75, k=7). In blinded studies it was 0.43 (0.02–0.85, k=4). In RCTs it was 0.46 (CI crossing zero, k=2). — [Mathiassen et al. 2025, moderator table](https://exa.ai/library/publication/0pp43g7tm02)

### Inferences
- Much of the subjective "memory worsening" reported in registries (16–26%) is plausibly tied to residual depression, expectations and how the question is framed. Because worsening was tied to remission status, a patient who does not respond is more likely to report memory loss and also less likely to benefit. That changes the risk-benefit calculation at the individual level.
- Historical figures from sine-wave ECT, fixed high-dose BL, or 1970s–1990s practice overstate risk for modern ultrabrief RUL ECT. Proponents also often borrow modern-technique reassurance for settings that still mainly use bitemporal ECT (see Section 4).
- Even after controlling for depression, the controlled autobiographical memory effect remains, so "it's just the depression" is not a sufficient explanation.
- Several risk factors apply directly to this patient. Younger age and female sex (if applicable) raise the risk of subjective worsening and of verbal-fluency decline. Older age, oddly, was associated with less AM loss. Prior heavy side-effect sensitivity is untested as a predictor.

### Gaps
- No high-quality data were found on benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, or polypharmacy as moderators of ECT cognitive outcomes in modern cohorts.
- The Brus et al. 2017 attribution is from memory and was not confirmed in the retrieved snippet.
- No study was found that measured premorbid cognition (for example, school or university records) in young ECT recipients to separate illness-related decline from treatment-related decline.

## 4. How much do results vary across centres and clinicians?

### Takeaway
A great deal. Sackeim's 7-hospital study found significant site differences in cognitive outcomes, both immediately and at 6 months, even after controlling for patient characteristics, and these were "largely attributable to differences in ECT technique". UK and Irish audit data show that bitemporal placement still dominates, at about 84–94% of courses at first treatment in ECTAS 2021–2022, and that "specific placements are used preferentially in many clinics". Systematic cognitive monitoring was patchy until recently. Scotland introduced a mandatory baseline cognition and autobiographical memory standard only in June 2023, and some boards were non-compliant. Published cognitive-risk figures therefore apply to *how* ECT is given, not to ECT as a single fixed treatment.

### Cited Findings
- Sackeim 2007: the hospitals did not differ at baseline, but differed in mMMS and AMI-SF scores post-ECT (mMMS F(6,333)=3.25, p=0.004; AMI-SF F(6,313)=2.70, p=0.01) and at 6 months (mMMS F(6,248)=3.43, p=0.003; AMI-SF F(6,240)=2.26, p=0.04). They also differed post-ECT on 5 other measures. "These differences among the hospitals were largely attributable to differences in ECT technique." — [Sackeim et al. 2007 full text](https://www.wisnerbaum.com/wp-content/uploads/23.-Sackeim-H.-et-al.2007.-The-cognitive-effects-of-ECT-in-community-settings.pdf)
- **ECTAS 2022** (England, Wales, Northern Ireland, Ireland; 90 member clinics): electrode placement at first treatment among depressive courses with outcome data was RUL 117, bitemporal 741 and bifrontal 25, so about 84% bitemporal. Unilateral ECT was used more often in first episodes (14.8%) than recurrent depression (12.0%) or bipolar disorder (8.7%), and "specific placements are used preferentially in many clinics." — [ECTAS 2022 dataset report](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/improving-care/ccqi/quality-networks/electro-convulsive-therapy-clinics-(ectas)/ectas-dataset-2022-report.pdf)
- **ECTAS 2021**: RUL 35, bitemporal 755 and bifrontal 17 courses in the remission sample, so about 94% bitemporal. Cognitive data submission became mandatory only from 2021. In 2020, when submission was optional, only 58% of patients had any documented cognitive score. — [ECTAS 2021 report](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/improving-care/ccqi/quality-networks/electro-convulsive-therapy-clinics-(ectas)/dataset-reports/ectas-2021-dataset-report.pdf?sfvrsn=59757c5c_5); [ECTAS 2020 report](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/improving-care/ccqi/quality-networks/electro-convulsive-therapy-clinics-(ectas)/ectas-2020-dataset-report-.pdf?sfvrsn=2b21c384_3)
- Mean treatments per acute course rose non-significantly from 9.3 (2012/13) to 10.1 (2021). Data sets were not published for 2018–2020. — [BJPsych Open 2024 review of ECTAS data](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/review-of-published-ectas-data-set-from-20122013-to-2021/3DF453AE0AB3836CD74B6C8C5C5C4568)
- In the 2016/17 ECTAS data, only 71 of an estimated 117 clinics (61%) submitted data. — [ECTAS 2016-17 dataset report](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/improving-care/ccqi/quality-networks/electro-convulsive-therapy-clinics-(ectas)/ectas-dataset-report-2016-17.pdf)
- **SEAN (Scotland) 2025 report**: 263 patients received 278 episodes, averaging about 10 treatments per acute episode, "broadly similar across Scotland". Standard 3 (baseline cognition and autobiographical memory before the first treatment) and Standard 7 (cognition reviewed at least every two treatments) were "introduced when the standards were updated in June 2023". NHS Tayside reported poor compliance because "cognition assessment had not previously been part of routine practice." — [SEAN 2025 annual report (Public Health Scotland, via Glasgow eprints)](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/394739/1/394739.pdf)
- The SEAN standards require repeat assessment of cognition and autobiographical memory 3 months after the last ECT. Clinicians may choose from tools including MoCA, ACE-III, 6CIT, the Autobiographical Memory Test and the AMI. — [SEAN Quality Improvement presentation, Langan Martin, May 2025](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/355484/2/355484.pdf)
- Historical SEAN 2016 data showed median treatments per episode ranging from 4 to 12 across Scottish hospitals. — [SEAN Annual Report 2017 (2016 data)](https://docslib.org/doc/3464786/sean-annual-report-2017)
- SEAN data for June–December 2023: 60% of episodes completed as planned; 53% of patients able to consent. — [NICE 2025 surveillance document](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta59/documents/consultation-document)
- Meta-analyses show RUL ECT is less detrimental to autobiographical memory (SMD 0.32 versus 0.82 for BL). Mathiassen et al. note that high-dose RUL has comparable antidepressant efficacy to BL. — [Mathiassen et al. 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/autobiographical-memory-after-electroconvulsive-therapy-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/8F694868E2767C11F75DBA2C341C21AF)

### Inferences
- For a UK patient, the "modern ECT is gentle" reassurance (ultrabrief RUL) may not match the default local practice, which is often bitemporal. Electrode placement, pulse width (ultrabrief at 0.25–0.3 ms versus brief at 0.5–1.0 ms), dosing method, treatment frequency (twice versus three times weekly) and number of sessions are negotiable variables with large cognitive consequences. A patient can ask a clinic for its own data.
- Because site and technique effects are large, population-average risk figures should not be applied uncritically to a specific clinic.

### Gaps
- No pulse-width or dosing-method distributions from ECTAS 2021/2022 were retrieved (the 2021 dataset added these questions, but the figures were not extracted).
- No Australian or Dutch registry data on practice variation were found in this search.
- The SEAN 2025 electrode-placement and side-effect percentages could not be extracted, because the PDF could not be fetched in full.

## 5. Does ECT cause structural brain damage? Imaging, white matter, biomarkers, neuropathology, animal studies

### Takeaway
No study at modern clinical parameters has found neuronal loss. Primate stereology, human post-mortem series and case reports, serum neuronal-injury markers (NfL, tau, S100B/NSE), and dementia cohorts all point the same way. MRI consistently shows *increases* in hippocampal and amygdala volume (dose-dependent, transient, mostly back to baseline by 3–6 months). Edema is not convincingly supported, and post-mortem and animal data suggest neuroplasticity: doublecortin, synaptogenesis, angiogenesis. However, several studies, including GEMRIC (n=435), link greater hippocampal enlargement, especially posterior, to *worse* cognitive side effects. DTI findings are mixed, including some mean-diffusivity increases possibly reflecting blood–brain barrier permeability, and GFAP shows a transient rise. So the effect is a real, temporary biological disturbance that can impair memory function without measurable cell death. "Not damage" does not mean "no functional harm".

### Cited Findings
**MRI volumetrics (GEMRIC and others)**
- GEMRIC mega-analysis (Oltedal et al. 2018, Biol Psychiatry): 281 patients from 10 sites plus 95 untreated controls scanned twice. Hippocampal enlargement depended on the number of ECT sessions and on electrode placement, "but volume change is not positively associated with clinical outcome." — [PubMed 30006199](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30006199/)
- Argyelan et al. 2021 (Transl Psychiatry): two independent cohorts (n=15 and n=14) plus 29 healthy controls to control for practice effects. ECT robustly increased hippocampal volume (t=5.07 and t=4.82, p<0.001). Greater enlargement correlated with worse neurocognitive change (r=−0.68, p=0.005, in one cohort), specifically for the hippocampus and not 41 other regions. — [Argyelan et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-021-01641-y)
- GEMRIC 2025 (Communications Medicine): 435 patients. Expansion of the hippocampal *body and tail* was associated with reduced verbal fluency (pFDR<0.05; cognitive data on n=124). Reduction of the tail by 6 months was associated with improved cognition (n=24). The authors suggest "there may be a threshold of ECT induced posterior hippocampal volumetric change, beyond which cognitive side effects occur." They cite earlier studies linking global hippocampal expansion to cognitive impairment. — [GEMRIC 2025, Commun Med, doi:10.1038/s43856-025-01120-1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-025-01120-1)
- GEMRIC 2024 pooled cognitive data: more treatments were associated with lower category verbal fluency with RUL placement. Otherwise "we did not observe ECT-mediated neurocognitive effects", but the authors acknowledge "the limited number of neuropsychological domains assessed". — [PubMed 39312853](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39312853/)
- Volumes are transient. Nordanskog et al. 2014 (n=12; 10 at 6 months, 7 at 12 months): hippocampal volume "returned to baseline during the follow-up period of 6 months". There was no FLAIR evidence of edema and "no persistent cognitive side effects were observed 1 year after ECT." — [Nordanskog et al. 2014, PMC4226425](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4226425/)
- Bracht et al. 2026 (J Affect Disord) meta-analysis: 15 studies (N=447) in the systematic review and 6 (N=151) in the meta-analysis. Left hippocampus rose +2.4% post-ECT and was +0.1% at follow-up; right rose +3.5% and was +0.8%. The baseline-to-follow-up change was not significant (g=0.07 and −0.06). — [Bracht et al. 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165032726001953)
- In 25 patients, CA4/dentate gyrus volume increased 1 week after ECT and "returned to baseline levels after 3 months irrespective of clinical state". Larger increases occurred in remitters. — [Acute and long-term effects of ECT on human dentate gyrus, PMC6785137](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6785137/)
- A long-term grey-matter study in Psychological Medicine 2023 agrees that grey-matter volume returns toward baseline by about 6 months. It notes only "a few… studies… covering an interval of at least 6 months… with samples including 23 or less ECT patients". — [Psychol Med 2023, doi:10.1017/S0033291723002647 (Ovid)](https://www.ovid.com/journals/pgme/fulltext/10.1017/s0033291723002647~long-term-effects-of-electroconvulsive-therapy-on-brain)
- Edema versus plasticity: quantitative MRI (myelin, iron, water) found "no brain tissue oedema contributing to the… hippocampus volume increase neither in the short, nor in the long-term", though the authors call this preliminary because the sample was small. — [Gyger et al. 2021, NeuroImage](https://isidore.science/index.php/document/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.117895)
- Other studies note reversible peri-ictal MRI signal changes, while "several studies report an absence of increased visible T2 signal or diffusivity changes following ECT, which argues against edema." — [Biophysical mechanisms of ECT-induced MTL volume expansion, PMC8474653](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8474653/)
- A smaller spectroscopy study (n=19) found no post-ECT change in metabolite concentrations and no spectral signature of neurogenesis. — [Jorgensen et al. 2016, Acta Psychiatr Scand](https://exa.ai/library/publication/c9d6qk82jr6)
- Another study reported a fall in hippocampal NAA/Cr, which it interpreted as possible "ECT-induced neural malfunction at the hippocampal level". — [Cano et al. 2017, Transl Psychiatry tp2016267](https://preview-www.nature.com/articles/tp2016267)

**White matter (DTI)**
- Lyden et al. 2014 (n=20 patients, 28 controls): fractional anisotropy (FA) rose and mean (MD) and radial diffusivity fell in fronto-limbic tracts, trending toward normal values and associated with response. Interpreted as increased fibre integrity. — [Lyden et al. 2014, Transl Psychiatry](https://www.nature.com/articles/tp201421)
- Repple et al. 2020 (Psychol Med; 29 ECT, 69 non-ECT inpatients, 52 healthy controls): ECT-specific *MD increase* in right-hemisphere tracts. Longer seizure duration was associated with FA decrease. The authors suggest this may reflect "increased permeability of the blood–brain barrier, resulting in disturbed communication of fibers"; the changes were unrelated to response. — [Repple et al., Psychol Med](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/influence-of-electroconvulsive-therapy-on-white-matter-structure-in-a-diffusion-tensor-imaging-study/8B2F9EB072CB44169EFB7F933E85B257)
- Belge et al. 2022 4-site harmonized mega-analysis (n=58): FA rose in the splenium and corticospinal tract, axial diffusivity rose in some tracts, and MD and radial diffusivity rose in overlapping regions. Framed as ECT "modulating"/"normalizing" white matter. Earlier studies were inconsistent: Nickl-Jockschat 2016 found no change. — [Belge et al. 2022, Transl Psychiatry](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-022-02284-3.pdf); [European Psychiatry abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-psychiatry/article/white-matter-changes-following-electroconvulsive-therapy-for-depression-a-megaanalysis/3EE88DEDF56818CAD3F7F971E9D20E28)

**Blood and CSF biomarkers of neuronal injury**
- Molecular Psychiatry 2024 (n=99 ECT patients, 99 age- and sex-matched controls): serum NfL did not change 30 minutes after the first ECT (+3.1%; 95% CI −0.5 to +6.7%) or before the sixth session (−3.2%). t-tau was also unchanged. GFAP rose transiently, suggesting astrocytic reactivity. At *baseline*, ECT patients already had higher NfL than controls (median 9.5 vs 7.0 pg/mL; +25.8%, p<0.001), as well as higher t-tau. Biomarkers did not explain long-term subjective memory impairment. — [Molecular Psychiatry 2024, doi:10.1038/s41380-024-02774-4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-024-02774-4)
- Besse et al. 2020 (World J Biol Psychiatry; n=15): serum NfL "remains unchanged during electroconvulsive therapy". A follow-up from the same Göttingen centre (n=15, 45 measurements, measured before, 24 hours after and 7 days after the course) found no change in NfL during transient cognitive side effects. — [PubMed 31818180](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31818180); ["The myth of brain damage…", PMC11226499](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11226499)
- Separately, NfL is elevated in major depression and correlates with executive dysfunction (40 patients vs 40 controls), which confounds any comparison of ECT-treated patients against healthy people. — [Chen et al. 2022, Int J Neuropsychopharmacol](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/25/2/99/6391491)

**Neuropathology and animal studies**
- Dwork et al. 2004 (Am J Psychiatry): 12 rhesus macaques received 6 weeks of daily bilateral ECT at 2.5 times seizure threshold, magnetic seizure therapy, or anaesthesia alone, with perfusion fixation and blinded neuropathology. "No identified lesions were attributable to the interventions." GFAP staining was most intense in the ECT group. The authors caution that the sample was "too small… to detect subtle, quantitative changes." — [Dwork et al. 2004, AJP](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.161.3.576)
- Dwork et al. 2009 (Neuroscience), unbiased stereology in 24 animals: "no effect… on volumes or total number or numerical density of neurons or glia in hippocampus, frontal cortex", limited to young healthy subjects. — [Dwork et al. 2009](https://www.ibroneuroscience.org/article/S0306-4522(09)01555-3/abstract)
- Human post-mortem cohort (Netherlands Brain Bank): patients given ECT within 5 years before death were compared with depressed patients without ECT and healthy controls. There was "no indications… for major hippocampal cell loss, overt cytoarchitectural changes or classic neuropathology" and no difference in astrocyte or microglia markers. Doublecortin and Stathmin-1 immunoreactivity (plasticity markers) were *higher* in the dentate gyrus of ECT patients. — [Loef et al. 2023 (preprint record)](https://exa.ai/library/publication/v6w7vvn3mqj); [earlier version](https://exa.ai/library/publication/3llk4z7q3z0)
- Case reports of brains after heavy ECT exposure include "Neuropathologic Examination After 91 ECT Treatments in a 92-Year-Old Woman" (Scalia et al., J ECT 2007;23:96–98) and "Neuropathological Evaluation of an 84-Year-Old Man After 422 ECT Treatments" (Anderson et al., J ECT 2014;30:248–250). Both are cited in the literature as showing no ECT-attributable damage. — [reference list in Loef et al. 2023](https://exa.ai/library/publication/v6w7vvn3mqj); [Dwork 2009 references](https://www.ibroneuroscience.org/article/S0306-4522(09)01555-3/abstract)
- Rat studies of electroconvulsive seizures in a genetic depression model: a single treatment increased hippocampal CA1 volume, microvessel length, mitochondria and synapse numbers. After 10 treatments these increases persisted at 3 months. Earlier work showed ECS-generated neurons survive for up to 12 months. — [Sustained ultrastructural changes after repeated ECS, PMC7387769](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7387769/)
- Proponents' framing: "cognitive changes, such as the amnestic effects of short-acting sedatives, can occur without cell loss or injury." — [Dwork et al. 2004](https://www.psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.161.3.576)
- Cumulative exposure: no association between lifetime ECT count (up to 186 sessions) and performance on verbal fluency, trail making, reaction time, complex figure and similar tests, although the tests may be insensitive (see Section 1). — [Kirov et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26585101/)
- The plaintiff in *Thelen* (95 ECTs) was diagnosed with a neurocognitive disorder. Courts made no causal finding. — [Thelen v. Somatics 2025](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-11th-circuit/117778022.html)

### Inferences
- Multiple independent methods agree that there is **no evidence of neuronal loss or classic neuropathology** at modern clinical parameters: primate stereology, human post-mortem tissue, ultrasensitive serum NfL and tau, MRI volume trajectories, and dementia cohorts. This is moderately strong evidence against "brain damage" in the sense of cell death.
- That is not the same as "biologically inert". ECT produces transient hippocampal swelling or remodelling, transient astrocytic activation (GFAP), and some white-matter diffusivity changes. The hippocampal changes correlate with memory side effects in several studies, including the largest one (GEMRIC n=435). The best-supported model is: **reversible neuroplastic disruption of the hippocampal memory network, causing a temporary anterograde deficit and permanent loss of some memories that were being consolidated, without detectable neuronal death.** Lost autobiographical memories can stay lost even if tissue recovers, because the forgetting is about failed consolidation or retrieval of specific traces, not shrinkage.
- The biomarker studies are reassuring about acute and subacute neuronal injury. Most sampled only during the course (up to session 6) or within 7 days of the end. They do not exclude very slow, low-level effects, though they make them less plausible.
- Primate and post-mortem studies are small, and many post-mortem subjects were elderly. Rare or subtle quantitative effects cannot be ruled out.

### Gaps
- Exact authorship of the Molecular Psychiatry 2024 biomarker study and of the Loef et al. post-mortem publication venue (journal) was not verified. Loef et al. was retrieved as preprint and library records.
- van Oostrom et al. 2018 (J ECT), on decreased cognitive functioning related to hippocampal enlargement, was not directly retrieved. It is presumably among the studies cited in GEMRIC 2025 refs 13–16.
- Devanand et al. 1994 ("Does ECT alter brain structure?", Am J Psychiatry) and the Kranaster / Zachrisson CSF and S100B studies were not directly retrieved in this search. Only NfL, tau and GFAP data were verified.
- No long-term (1 year or more) DTI follow-up studies were found.
- No study was found specifically in young adults (under 30) examining structural or biomarker outcomes.

## 6. Dementia risk after ECT

### Takeaway
Six large registry cohorts (Denmark ×2, Taiwan, Wales and others), pooled in a 2025 GRADE-assessed meta-analysis of 286,995 patients, show **no increased dementia risk overall** after adjustment (HR 0.98; 95% CI 0.79–1.21; very low certainty). Risk is *lower* in people over 50 (HR 0.78; moderate certainty). Crude rates are higher in ECT recipients, but the difference disappears once confounding by illness severity is handled. A caution signal exists in younger people: in Wales, people aged 50 or under had a nominally significant HR of 1.40 (1.08–1.82), and Denmark showed a non-significant HR of 1.51 for under-50s. Both are likely residual confounding but deserve mention for a young patient.

### Cited Findings
- **Osler et al. 2018** (Lancet Psychiatry 5:348–356, doi:10.1016/S2215-0366(18)30056-7): 168,015 Danish patients with a first hospital diagnosis of affective disorder (2005–2015), of whom 5,901 (3.5%) received ECT; median follow-up 4.9 years. Crude dementia rates were 3.6% with ECT versus 3.1% without (70.4 vs 59.2 per 10,000 person-years). Age-adjusted HRs: under 50, 1.51 (0.67–3.46); 50–69, 1.15 (0.91–1.47); 70 and over, 0.68 (0.58–0.80), attenuating to 0.77 (0.59–1.00) after propensity matching. Accounting for competing mortality gave a sub-distribution HR of 0.98 (0.76–1.26). Dementia was defined by hospital diagnosis or acetylcholinesterase-inhibitor use. — [Lancet Psychiatry abstract](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpsy/article/PIIS2215-0366(18)30056-7/abstract); [PubMed 29523431](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29523431/)
- In the same study's Danish Conscription Database subsample (13,578 men; 6.8% received ECT), lower cognitive ability at age 19 was associated with higher dementia incidence. Authors suggested "those with fewer cognitive reserves are more susceptible to the adverse cognitive effects of ECT". This was reported in media coverage, not verified in primary text. — [Medscape 2018](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/894022)
- A commentary noted that in patients aged 70 and over, more than 10 ECT sessions were associated with lower dementia incidence (HR 0.54; 0.36–0.80), and that the reliability of the registry dementia diagnosis is "unsure". — [Evidence-Based Mental Health commentary, PMC10270403](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10270403/)
- **Hjerrild et al. 2021** (J ECT 37:250–255): 1,089 Aarhus ECT patients (1982–2000) matched to 3,011 non-ECT inpatients and 108,867 people from the general population, with up to 34 years of follow-up. Cumulative dementia incidence was 13.45% (ECT), 10.53% (non-ECT) and 8.43% (background). With ECT as reference, the adjusted HR for the non-ECT group was 0.73 (0.52–1.04), not significant. — [Hjerrild et al. 2021 record](https://exa.ai/library/publication/30t2n8kgy76)
- Kirov et al. re-express this as an ECT HR of 1.37 (0.96–1.92). — [Kirov et al. 2025, PMC12404881](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12404881/)
- **Chu et al. 2018** (Front Psychiatry; Taiwan; 994 ECT patients vs 2,982 controls, 46.7% with schizophrenia; 10-year follow-up): 4.53% versus 5.0% developed dementia; adjusted HR 0.633 (0.448–1.895). Within the ECT cohort, prolonged seizures, in-hospital delirium and acute stroke were associated with higher dementia risk. — [Chu et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2018.00397/full)
- **Kirov et al. 2025** (Acta Psychiatr Scand 152:270–277, doi:10.1111/acps.70005; whole-population Wales): 110,774 people followed for a mean of 24.5 years. Crude dementia rates were 15.4% (ECT) versus 13.1%. The adjusted HR was 0.888 (0.757–1.044). Among those aged 51 and over, HR was 0.741 (0.60–0.91). Among those aged 50 or under, **HR 1.399 (1.08–1.82), p=0.012**, which the authors frame as a trend replicating Osler's non-significant under-50 finding. All-cause mortality was lower with ECT (HR 0.81). — [Kirov et al. 2025, PMC12404881](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12404881/)
- **2025 GRADE-assessed meta-analysis** (literature to July 2025): 6 observational cohorts, 286,995 patients (9,124 ECT-exposed), follow-up 4.9–16.9 years. Pooled HR 0.98 (0.79–1.21; I²=67.1%; **very low certainty**). Excluding one study gave HR 0.87 (p=0.045). In those over 50 (n=112,842), HR 0.78 (0.69–0.88; I²=0%; moderate certainty). The authors call for "proper adjustment for potential confounders." — [PubMed 41176476](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41176476/)
- A 2026 BJPsych review of administrative-data studies: "before confounding was considered, there was a higher incidence of dementia among those receiving ECT… once confounding was accounted for all studies found that there was no indication ECT increased the risk… except for the younger age subgroup in one study, which had few events and a small sample size." — [BJPsych 2026 review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/electroconvulsive-therapy-improved-understanding-of-longterm-risks-and-benefits-from-advances-in-administrative-health-data/AD25574181FBFE50BAED6400DF54EC72)
- Older small studies (n=81 and n=47) suggesting increased dementia after ECT were described as methodologically flawed. — [Chu et al. 2018 discussion](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2018.00397/full); [PMC10270403](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10270403/)
- In a small Swedish cohort (n=49, mean age 61), the 17 who later developed dementia had lower cerebral blood flow and EEG frequency *before* ECT and more disorientation after it. This suggests prodromal disease predicts adverse ECT cognitive reactions, rather than ECT causing dementia. — [Berggren et al. 2016, J Affect Disord 200:15–24](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/d3245b3e-0b58-4a22-9427-40748ab1661c)

### Inferences
- The confounding problem cuts both ways. Severe depression is itself a dementia risk factor, which inflates crude ECT associations. Prodromal dementia may present as late-life depression and lead to ECT referral, which inflates them further. "Healthier" or more closely monitored patients may be chosen for ECT, which deflates them. The protective-looking HR in older adults may reflect selection and competing mortality, not true protection.
- For a young adult, the reassuring older-age data are less directly relevant. The only signal pointing upward comes from people under 50 (Wales HR 1.40; Denmark HR 1.51, n.s.). It is most plausibly residual confounding: young ECT recipients are a highly selected, chronically ill group with more comorbidity, including substance use. With few events, it cannot be dismissed outright.
- Registry studies measure diagnosed dementia, a coarse endpoint that cannot detect milder persistent cognitive change.

### Gaps
- No Swedish nationwide ECT–dementia cohort was located in this search, and the six cohorts in the 2025 meta-analysis were not individually confirmed.
- Authors and journal of the 2025 GRADE meta-analysis (PMID 41176476) were not retrieved.
- No study was found that specifically examined dementia or mild cognitive impairment in people who received ECT before age 30, with decades of follow-up.

## 7. Overall verdict: GRADE-style certainty

### Takeaway
(a) Transient cognitive effects are certain and well quantified. (b) Persistent anterograde or general cognitive impairment with modern technique is unlikely at group level but poorly excluded for individuals and subtle domains. (c) Persistent loss of some autobiographical memories is real, larger with bilateral placement, and not regained by 6–12 months on current evidence, though its absolute size and functional impact are uncertain. (d) Structural brain damage in the sense of neuronal loss is not supported by any modern evidence, while transient, reversible hippocampal and glial changes are real and may underlie the memory effects. The commonly quoted figures are **neither simply inflated nor simply understated**. Reassuring figures are understated for retrograde memory and for individuals. Alarming figures are inflated for "brain damage" and dementia, and partly inflated by depression-driven complaints and outdated technique.

### Cited Findings
- (a) Acute and subacute deficits: 72% of cognitive variables declined at 0–3 days, with effect sizes up to −1.10, recovering at group level by 4–15 days. — [Semkovska & McLoughlin 2010](https://www.biologicalpsychiatryjournal.com/article/S0006-3223(10)00598-6/abstract)
- (a) In a modern RCT, ELEKT-D (Anand et al. 2023, NEJM; 365 patients with TRD; ECT three times a week versus IV ketamine), ketamine patients had higher memory-recall scores than ECT patients (p<0.05). ECT was "associated with memory impairment that recovered gradually". — [PCORI ELEKT-D results](https://www.pcori.org/research-results/2016/comparing-ketamine-versus-electroconvulsive-therapy-patients-treatment-resistant-depression-elekt-d-study); [NEJM Journal Watch](https://clinician.nejm.org/ketamine-vs-ect-patients-treatment-resistant-depression-nejm-jw.NA56185)
- (b) No persistent group-level anterograde deficit beyond 15 days, with improvement over baseline in 57% of variables. Retrograde memory was not tested, quality was not assessed, and the baseline was depressed. — [NCBI/DARE summary](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK80031/?report=printable)
- (b) Sine-wave stimulation caused persistent reaction-time slowing at 6 months (historical technique). — [Sackeim et al. 2007](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf)
- (b) No cumulative deficit across up to 186 sessions on standard tests, with the authors conceding these tests may miss subtle deficits. — [Kirov et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26585101/); [Authors' reply](https://exa.ai/library/publication/nydyj523xtz)
- (c) Controlled meta-analysis: SMD 0.55 overall (RUL 0.32, BL 0.82); stable or not regained by 6–12 months in 4 studies; publication bias present. — [Mathiassen et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40357797/)
- (c) 12.4% had marked and persistent retrograde amnesia at 6 months in a mixed-technique community sample, driven by BL. — [Sackeim et al. 2007](https://power2u.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/Sackheim-2007.pdf)
- (c) Subjective worsening was 16% at about 2.5 months (Sigström 2020) and 25% at 6 months (Tornhamre 2025), confounded by residual depression, expectations and question framing. — [Sigström et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7176828); [Tornhamre et al. 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S016503272402007X)
- (d) No neuronal loss in primates (stereology) or in human post-mortem hippocampi; no NfL or tau rise; transient GFAP rise; transient hippocampal enlargement correlated with cognitive side effects. — [Dwork et al. 2009](https://www.ibroneuroscience.org/article/S0306-4522(09)01555-3/abstract); [Loef et al. 2023](https://exa.ai/library/publication/v6w7vvn3mqj); [Mol Psychiatry 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-024-02774-4); [GEMRIC 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-025-01120-1)
- Dementia: HR 0.98 (0.79–1.21), very low certainty overall; HR 0.78 in people over 50, moderate certainty. — [PubMed 41176476](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41176476/)

### Inferences
These are my GRADE-style judgements, synthesised from the findings above.

- **(a) Transient cognitive effects** (post-ictal disorientation, anterograde amnesia, impaired new learning and verbal fluency during the course and for up to about 2–4 weeks after): **HIGH certainty they occur.** **MODERATE certainty** that group means recover by about 2 weeks. Inconsistency between tests and the possibility of a subgroup recovering more slowly prevent a "high" rating for recovery timing. Practical consequence: a course plus recovery needs roughly 1–2 months away from demanding study, timed outside exam or application periods.
- **(b) Persistent anterograde or general cognitive impairment (beyond 1–6 months) with modern brief or ultrabrief RUL technique:** **LOW certainty of no clinically meaningful persistent effect.** Downgraded for indirectness (insensitive screening tests, depressed baseline, little functional or academic outcome data), imprecision, and risk of bias (attrition, short follow-up, no non-ECT control arms). There is **moderate certainty that sine-wave stimulation caused persistent psychomotor slowing**, which is historical but relevant if any clinic still uses wide pulses. There is **very low certainty** about vulnerable individuals; side-effect-prone patients with prominent brain fog are not studied as a subgroup.
- **(c) Persistent retrograde / autobiographical memory loss:** **MODERATE certainty** that ECT causes some loss of autobiographical memories, especially for the months before and during treatment, that is not regained by 6–12 months. It is about 2.5 times larger with bilateral than RUL placement (SMD 0.82 vs 0.32; NNH about 5 for BL vs RUL). Downgraded from high for small, mostly observational studies, a contested instrument without norms, and publication bias. Upgraded for a dose–response gradient (number of BL treatments, pulse width) and consistency across research groups including former critics of the tool. **LOW certainty** about prevalence of severe, functionally disabling loss (somewhere from about 1 in 8 under older mixed-technique practice to "rare" with ultrabrief RUL). **VERY LOW certainty** about losses extending beyond 1 year back, about memory for learned semantic or academic material, and about outcomes beyond 12 months.
- **(d) Structural brain damage (neuronal loss, lesions):** **MODERATE certainty that it does not occur** at modern clinical parameters. Primate stereology, post-mortem tissue, serum NfL and tau, reversible MRI trajectories and dementia cohorts all converge, but each line is small or indirect. **LOW certainty about subtle microstructural effects** (white-matter diffusivity changes, transient astrocytic activation, possible threshold effects of posterior hippocampal expansion). These are best described as reversible biological perturbation, not proven injury.
- **Dementia:** **LOW certainty of no increased risk** across ages (observational evidence; GRADE authors rated it very low). **Moderate certainty of no increase** in people over 50. **Very low certainty** for people under 30 to 50, where one Welsh analysis showed an upward signal.
- **Net answer to "robust, inflated or understated?"**
  - The typical patient-information line, "memory problems usually clear within weeks", is **robust for anterograde memory** but **understates** retrograde autobiographical loss and individual variation.
  - Patient surveys giving 29–55% "permanent memory loss", and phrases like "brain damage", **overstate prevalence and mechanism**. They are self-selected samples confounded by depression and expectations, and there is no neuronal loss.
  - The figure most applicable to a carefully delivered modern course comes from controlled studies: a small-to-moderate, probably permanent, gap in autobiographical memory around the treatment period with RUL, larger with bilateral.
  - Risk depends strongly on technique: electrode placement, pulse width, number and frequency of sessions, and the clinic.
- **For this patient:**
  - **Technique choices.** Ultrabrief-pulse RUL, titrated dosing, twice-weekly sessions and limiting the total number of sessions all minimise risk. The patient should ask what the clinic routinely uses and whether bitemporal is the default.
  - **Monitoring.** A comprehensive baseline neuropsychological battery that includes autobiographical memory gives an individual reference point for follow-up at 2 and 6 months.
  - **Timing.** Avoid courses that overlap with critical learning periods, and record important recent personal and academic information before starting.

- Scite Smart Citation tools could not be used: the server returned "MCP tools require a paid scite plan or an active free trial".
